# Prior art and the design space {#ch5}

The idea of hiding who talks to whom is older than any blockchain, and Midnight inherits most of the techniques. Encryption hides what a message says but not who sent it, who receives it, when, how often or how large it is. These facts are the message's metadata, and each technique below hides some part of them from some party. The techniques fall into nine families by mechanism: flooding with trial decryption; DC-nets; mixnets; onion routing; private information retrieval; receiver-side detection; decentralized publish/subscribe overlays, GossipSub among them; deployed messengers; and ledger-based carriers with stealth addressing. The privacy terms and adversary classes are those of Chapter 4.

Figure 5.1 places the families on two axes: what a scheme hides, from content alone up to sender and recipient hidden from a global observer, and what the hiding costs and who pays it.

{{fig:5-1}}

No family is both cheap and strongly private. Flooding, DC-nets and mixnets sit at the top of the hiding axis because every participant, or every message, pays for traffic that is not its own. Topic-based gossip sits at the bottom of both axes: it is cheap because it routes by interest, and routing by interest exposes interest. Receiver-side detection and private information retrieval occupy the middle. They move the cost without removing it, either to the receiver, who downloads more than it needs, or to a server that computes over everything.

The words "event" and "private event" keep the Foundation's meaning throughout. An event is a contract event emitted by a Compact circuit and recorded in a transaction [@midnight-mip-0002]. A private event is the planned contract event with encrypted, commitment-bound fields [@midnight-mps-0005]. The objects Midnight Express carries are Messages. A Publisher seals each Message into an MPE Envelope of one of four fixed sizes; traffic is divided into Shards, and a Subscriber receives a whole Shard and picks out the MPE Envelopes of the streams it follows (Chapter 1).

## Bitmessage and flooding with trial decryption

Bitmessage, proposed in 2012, sends every message to every node in a *stream*. Each node tries its private keys on every object it receives, and only the node whose key opens a message can read it [@2012-warren-bitmessage-whitepaper]. This is *trial decryption*: a node cannot tell which objects are its own, so it tries each one. The sender pays for admission with a hash-based proof of work on each object. It searches for a nonce that makes a hash of the object fall below a target, and the only known way to find one is to try many, so junk is costly to send before any relay inspects it. The whitepaper put the sending cost at "an average of four minutes of work" on an average computer [@2012-warren-bitmessage-whitepaper, p. 2]. The protocol fixes the difficulty through two parameters, `nonceTrialsPerByte` and `payloadLengthExtraBytes`, each with a network minimum of 1,000, and caps an object at 2^18 bytes and its expiry at 28 days and 3 hours [@2012-bitmessage-protocol-specification]. Difficulty scales with payload length and time to live, so long-lived spam costs proportionally more [@2015-schaub-bitmessage-antispam].

Bitmessage hides content from everyone without the key, and the recipient from relays and observers, because every node receives every object in the stream. The set of participants who could, for all an adversary can tell, be a message's recipient is its *anonymity set*; in Bitmessage it is every node in the stream. The whitepaper's stated aim was to "mask non-content data, like the sender and receiver of messages, from those not involved" [@2012-warren-bitmessage-whitepaper, p. 1].

Every node carries the whole stream and performs one trial decryption per object per identity. Only expiry bounds storage: the whitepaper had nodes keep objects for two days, and a user offline for n days had to stay online for n days to catch up [@2012-warren-bitmessage-whitepaper, pp. 3-4]. The proof of work falls hardest on the weakest devices. Schaub et al. assume about 5 MH/s for a desktop, 0.5 MH/s for a phone and 30 MH/s for a spam bot, and call the scheme "unfair towards users with less powerful hardware" [@2015-schaub-bitmessage-antispam, p. 3].

Bitmessage fails in five ways.

- Growth was met by splitting the network into streams, and each split shrinks the set of possible recipients [@2012-warren-bitmessage-whitepaper, p. 2].
- Timing leaks location. Warren conceded that an acknowledgement first seen leaving one machine reveals the recipient's location [@2012-warren-bitmessage-whitepaper, p. 4].
- Forward secrecy appears in the protocol only as a service bit whose "exact design is pending" [@2012-bitmessage-protocol-specification].
- An eval-injection flaw let any sender on the network run code on a recipient's machine [@2018-nvd-cve-2018-1000070-pybitmessage].
- Spam control by rate limit is impractical, because "the sender of the messages is encrypted and therefore only known to the recipient" [@2015-schaub-bitmessage-antispam, p. 3].

**The heirs.** Whisper took the same flood-and-try design into Ethereum. It added a 4-byte topic and a 512-bit Bloom filter of interests, a bit array that answers "possibly present" or "definitely absent" for an item with a tunable rate of false "possibly" answers, so that a node could reveal its topics "without compromising (too much) privacy" [@2017-eip-627-whisper]. Waku v1 forked Whisper and dropped its proof of work. Its own security notes say proof of work "is a poor spam prevention mechanism" on mobile devices, and that light nodes and mail servers learn topic interests together with IP addresses [@2019-vac-waku1-spec]. Swarm's Trojan chunks make a message indistinguishable from any other encrypted 4 KB chunk, but the sender must mine a nonce whose expected cost equals the network size. The same document records that Whisper "never saw wide adoption due to its (obvious) lack of scalability" [@2021-tron-book-of-swarm, pp. 172-176].

Bitmessage established four ideas that Midnight Express keeps: relays carry sealed objects whose content and recipient they cannot read, recipients recognize their own traffic locally, objects expire, and admission is paid per object. It also fixed what recipient privacy costs: whoever wants it receives everything in the recipient set. Midnight Express keeps that property inside a Shard, where a Subscriber downloads the whole Shard and tests every MPE Envelope. At the baseline mix of size classes (60, 25, 10 and 5 percent for classes 0 to 3, a mean wire length of 2,158.4 B) and 10 MPE Envelopes per second, that is 10 × 2,158.4 B × 86,400 s, about 1.86 GB per day (derived; Chapter 9). The ceiling at the per-Shard byte cap is 65,536 B/s × 86,400 s, about 5.7 GB per day.

Midnight Express does not keep Bitmessage's proof of work. A cost in CPU time does not bind an identity, and it punishes phones. The design binds admission to a membership in the Bus Registry contract instead (Chapter 8). In the proof of concept the Admission Proof will be a stand-in whose secrets the relays can see, so the binding will be exercised there but will not be private.

Sharding repeats Bitmessage's stream split at the same privacy cost, since more Shards mean smaller recipient sets. One stream of 1,000 nodes hides each recipient among 1,000; four streams of 250 hide each among 250.

## DC-nets, mixnets, onion routing and private information retrieval

Three lineages aim at the strongest properties of Chapter 4: sender anonymity, receiver anonymity and relationship unobservability, all against a global passive observer, which sees every link in the network but sends nothing.

**DC-nets.** Every participant broadcasts, and the XOR of all broadcasts reveals the messages without revealing who sent each one. Take three participants, each pair sharing a secret random string (k_AB for A and B, and so on). A, who has a message m, broadcasts k_AB ⊕ k_AC ⊕ m; B broadcasts k_AB ⊕ k_BC; C broadcasts k_AC ⊕ k_BC. Each key appears in exactly two broadcasts and cancels, so the XOR of all three is m, and neither an observer nor B or C alone can tell who added it. The same arithmetic shows the weakness: anyone can XOR garbage into a broadcast, and nothing in the garbled output says who did. A survey of the branch notes that a single dishonest insider "can straightforwardly jam the network" [@2022-shirali-dcnetsurvey, p. 15].

The guarantee is information-theoretic, holding even against unlimited computing power, but its cost grows with the group. Dissent measured 1 MB messages taking under a minute in a 4-member group and 14 minutes in a 40-node group [@2010-corrigangibbs-dissent, p. 2]. Moving the work onto servers trusted under an *anytrust* assumption (at least one server honest) took Dissent to 5,000 participants, with latencies as low as 600 ms for 600-client groups [@2012-wolinsky-dissentnumbers, p. 1]. Verdict made disruption traceable and ran 1,000 clients at 10 seconds per exchange [@2013-corrigangibbs-verdict, p. 2]. PriFi brought the overhead to about 100 ms for 100 clients, but only on a local network [@2017-barman-prifi]. Riposte applies the idea to anonymous writes into a shared table: it handled 32.8 writes per second into a 65,536-row table and needed an 11-hour epoch to gather an anonymity set of about a million users [@2015-corrigangibbs-riposte, pp. 2, 17].

**Mixnets.** A mixnet passes layered-encrypted packets through a chain of relays. Each relay removes one layer, which tells it only the next hop, and forwards what it holds in a new order, so an observer cannot match outgoing to incoming packets by bytes or position. Delay makes the shuffle work, since a relay that forwards at once can be matched by timing. Cover traffic, dummy packets indistinguishable from real ones, hides whether a user is sending at all.

Sphinx, the standard packet format, encodes a five-hop header in about 224 bytes [@2009-danezis-sphinx]. Loopix mixes continuously. It adds Poisson delays, random holding times that keep no memory of when a packet arrived, and three kinds of cover traffic; one relay handles more than 300 messages per second with under 1.5 ms of processing overhead [@2017-piotrowska-loopix, p. 1]. The chosen delays set end-to-end latency, about two seconds per hop in its evaluation. Nym deploys the Loopix design with token incentives [@2021-diaz-nym].

Synchronous systems batch all users at fixed intervals and pad with differentially private noise: dummy messages in random amounts, so that what an observer counts barely depends on any one user. Vuvuzela carries 68,000 messages per second for a million users at 37 seconds end-to-end latency. Each client uses about 12 KB/s whether it talks or not, 30 GB a month, and each server about 166 MB/s [@2015-vandenhooff-vuvuzela, pp. 1-2]. Its successors trade latency for scale. Alpenhorn's dialling takes 150 seconds for 10 million users [@2016-lazar-alpenhorn], and Stadium serves 20 million users at two minutes [@2017-tyagi-stadium]. Karaoke reports 6.8 seconds for 2 million users, but assumes 80 percent of servers are honest instead of one [@2018-lazar-karaoke].

A libp2p mix protocol now specifies Sphinx packets of 4,608 bytes over at least three hops [@2024-vac-libp2p-mix-spec]. Waku applies it to publishing and history queries only, recommends at least 100 mix nodes, and states that subscriber anonymity "is not addressed" [@2025-waku-mix-spec].

**Onion routing.** Onion routing gives up the global observer to stay interactive. Tor sends each connection through a circuit of relays, each of which removes one layer of encryption, and to keep latency low it does not obscure packet timings or sizes [@2015-sun-raptor]. It hides a client's address from the destination and from any single relay, but an adversary who observes both ends of a connection can link them. Autonomous systems can use the asymmetry and churn of Internet routing, and BGP hijacks and interceptions, to observe both ends for more users [@2015-sun-raptor], and a low-resource attacker can take full control of the information flows of users who reach a peer-to-peer network over Tor, Bitcoin in that study [@2014-biryukov-bitcointor]. Submission over an onion-routing network would hide a Publisher's address from the ingress Bus Node at the latency of a relay circuit, and would leave the timing and size residuals of Chapter 4.

**Private information retrieval.** PIR lets a client retrieve a record without the server learning which one. With two servers that do not collude, a simple scheme suffices. To retrieve bit i, the client sends a random subset S of positions to one server and S with position i toggled to the other, and each server returns the XOR of the bits it was asked for. The XOR of the two answers is bit i, and each server sees only a uniformly random subset [@1998-chor-pir]. The privacy rests on the servers not comparing notes.

For one server the same paper gives the bound: with information-theoretic privacy, "the whole database should be down-loaded" [@1998-chor-pir, p. 1]. Computational PIR escapes the bound at a CPU cost. SimplePIR answers at about 10 GB/s per core but needs a 121 MB client hint for a 1 GB database [@2022-henzinger-simplepir].

Mailbox systems apply PIR to messaging. Pung delivers one message in about 1.3 seconds against a million records, and its clients spend 3.8 to 11 MB of network traffic per message [@2016-angel-pung-1]. Addra reaches a p99 of 726 ms for 32,000 users, at 1.46 Mbps of download per client [@2021-ahmad-addra]. Talek serves 32,000 users at 1.7 seconds with 148 MB per client per day, and states that it does not hide when users are online [@2020-cheng-talek]. With two servers, Express cuts the client cost to about 5 KB per message [@2021-eskandarian-express].

2PPS, the only retrieval-based scheme reviewed here that targets publish/subscribe directly, combines secret-shared writes with multi-server PIR reads. It serves 100,000 clients at 5 seconds latency. Every client must act in every period, about 54 percent of what clients receive is cover, and the authors concede vulnerability to intersection attacks [@2021-2pps-pubsubprivacy].

**The bound that ties them together.** Das et al. prove that an anonymous communication protocol can have only two of three properties: strong anonymity, low bandwidth overhead and low latency overhead. If only a fraction β of users send noise in each time step, and a message may stay in the network for at most ℓ steps, strong anonymity is impossible whenever 2βℓ < 1, even with every party honest [@2017-das-trilemma, pp. 1-2]. If 1 percent of users send noise in each step (β = 0.01), messages must be allowed to wait at least 50 steps (2 × 0.01 × ℓ ≥ 1 gives ℓ ≥ 50); meeting the condition is necessary, not sufficient. Protocols in which users coordinate, such as DC-nets, escape the condition only by having every user send a share for every real message [@2020-das-comprehensivetrilemma]. A later survey reads the bound as covering onion routing and mixing rather than DC-nets [@2020-kuhn-sokperformance], with the same practical verdict: all formal analyses found "a prohibitively high overhead" necessary for provable anonymity. Sasy and Goldberg's systematization agrees for each family it surveys. DC-nets "are inherently synchronous, and have poor scalability", and PIR families scale poorly across servers [@2023-sasy-sokmetadata, p. 11].

For Midnight Express, the trilemma removes one option at the outset. Midnight Express sends no cover traffic (β = 0) and targets delivery within seconds, so 2βℓ = 0 < 1 whatever the delay. On the trilemma's terms it cannot offer sender anonymity against a global observer, and it does not claim to (Chapter 4).

The lineage remains the only route to that property, and its cost is concrete. Vuvuzela's 12 KB/s per idle client is about 1 GB per day (12 KB/s × 86,400 s), the same order as the 1.86 GB per day a Subscriber downloads for a whole Shard at the baseline mix. The operating demands are as concrete: all users online at fixed intervals, a set of servers of which one, or most, must be honest, and latency of seconds to minutes. Option 4 in Chapter 6 evaluates what a mixnet ingress or a PIR delivery path would add on top of the overlay. The libp2p mix specification is the most direct candidate, because it shares the transport stack.

## Fuzzy detection and oblivious message retrieval

The schemes in this family keep Bitmessage's goal of hiding the recipient without downloading everything. A *detector*, a server that examines all the traffic on the receiver's behalf, passes on a smaller set without learning which messages are the receiver's. To filter, it must know something about the receiver, and what it knows it can use.

**Foundations.** The ancestor is searchable encryption. Boneh et al. let a gateway test whether an encrypted mail carries a keyword "without learning anything else" [@2004-boneh-peks]. Two results bound the family. A ciphertext can avoid revealing which public key it was made for, a property called key privacy that ElGamal-style encryption has and RSA does not [@2001-bellare-keyprivacy]. And no public-key scheme, "no matter how strong", prevents an adversary from making selective trial deliveries to a suspected recipient and watching the reaction [@2013-kohlweiss-anonymitypke].

**Fuzzy message detection (FMD).** The sender attaches a *clue*. The receiver gives a detector a key that flags all of its own messages plus a random fraction p of everyone else's, where p = 2^-n. The detector cannot tell true matches from false ones, but it learns the size of each user's flagged set. At p = 2^-8, a Shard carrying 864,000 MPE Envelopes a day would flag about 3,375 other people's MPE Envelopes per receiver (864,000 / 256), and the detector learns that the receiver's own are among those few thousand.

Penumbra deploys a variant in which the network, not the receiver, fixes p, since a per-user rate would make users distinguishable by their rates. Supporting rates down to 2^-14 needed 448-byte keys before Penumbra's compaction [@2022-penumbra-fmd-spec]. Penumbra's own notes warn that a detector holding several keys "can empirically link the corresponding diversified addresses", and recommend spreading keys across detectors [@2022-penumbra-protocol-detection-memo].

A statistical attack summarized by Goes et al. sets the limit. With about a million users and p ≈ 2^-8, a detection server clusters related messages with probability about 0.5, and pools of ten million messages need p above about 2^-6 [@2025-goes-multiserverfmd, pp. 2-4]. Their remedy splits detection across three servers with an honest majority, so each server sees a rate of 2^-6 while the receiver's own rate is about 2^-18. Madathil et al. put numbers on the trade-off. At ρ = 2^-15 and 500,000 messages, the anonymity set is about 15; at ρ = 2^-5 it is 15,625, but detection takes up to 9 hours [@2021-madathil-privatesignaling, p. 22].

**Oblivious message retrieval (OMR).** The detector runs under fully homomorphic encryption, which lets a party compute on data it cannot read, and returns a compact encrypted digest of the receiver's messages. It learns nothing, even if every party except the receiver colludes [@2021-liu-omr]. Because it cannot tell which messages match, it must process every message for every receiver it serves.

In the original scheme, detection costs about 0.065 seconds per message, or about $1 per million messages, for each receiver served. The receiver uploads a 129 MB detection key once and decodes 50 messages out of 500,000 in about 20 ms, and each clue is about 956 bytes [@2021-liu-omr; @2024-liu-perfomr]. PerfOMR brings detection down to about 7.9 ms per message ($0.12 per million) [@2024-liu-perfomr], and SophOMR reduces both running time and digest size again [@2024-lee-sophomr]. OMR with strong unlinkability of detection keys is at least as hard as PIR, and UnifOMR handles 2^19 messages in about 25 seconds and 4 MB of communication [@2026-fisch-unifomr]. Signalling schemes built on enclaves take under 70 ms per sent message and under 6 seconds per retrieval for a million recipients [@2023-jakkamsetti-scalablesignaling], but their trust moves to the enclave vendor.

**Filters and tags.** Light clients, which ask a server for what they need, have long used cheaper schemes with a poor privacy record. A Bitcoin light client sends a server a Bloom filter of its addresses and receives the transactions that test "possibly present". Once two filters are collected, such clients leak almost all addresses of a user who holds fewer than about 20, whatever false-positive rate is set [@2014-gervais-bloomfilters, p. 1]. Compact block filters reverse the direction: the server publishes a filter per block and the client downloads whole blocks [@2017-bip157-clientfilters].

Monero's view tag puts one byte of a shared-secret hash on each output, so a scanning wallet skips about 255 of 256 outputs after one hash. The measured saving is 30 to 40 percent of scanning time, and the tag leaks at most 8 bits [@2022-monero-pr8061-viewtags]. Zcash's light-client protocol trims each output to 116 bytes for trial decryption, an 80 percent bandwidth saving, but the client still tests every output [@2018-grigg-zip307-light-client]. Aztec derives a fresh tag per message as `poseidon2(secret, index)` from a pairwise secret and a counter, and its nodes index logs by tag. The documentation names the leak: the node "can observe your IP address and correlate it with which tags … you're interested in". It calls OMR and PIR "currently impractical in production" [@2024-aztec-private-events-note-discovery].

Midnight's own proposals take the filtering route. MIP-0002, the one Accepted event proposal, lets Indexer clients query standard events by a byte prefix of an indexed field, so that the application can "choose the level of disclosure at the cost of the data returned"; it cites Monero's view tags [@midnight-mip-0002]. MPS-0005 (Proposed) states the problem for private events: "Fully encrypted events without metadata force wallets to download and trial-decrypt every event on chain". It plans topic-based filtering with a claimed "99%+ scanning reduction" [@midnight-mps-0005], and its planned Indexer items (a topics column, "wallet-specific decryption with session isolation" and a per-wallet relevance table) place the relevance decision at the Indexer. MPS-0005 does not state what the filtering party learns. Its Part 2 text is planned and not yet published, so the key types, the topic derivation and the Indexer's knowledge are unknown.

For Midnight Express, one Shard at 10 MPE Envelopes per second carries 864,000 MPE Envelopes per day (10 × 86,400). Original OMR would spend about 56,000 seconds of detector time per receiver per day (864,000 × 0.065 s), which is 15.6 hours; PerfOMR about 1.9 hours (864,000 × 7.9 ms). An OMR clue (about 956 B) is also larger than the whole 776-byte wire length of the smallest MPE Envelope class, so it would break the fixed size classes that hide payload length.

FMD is cheaper to run, but at the rates that save bandwidth it leaks the receiver's candidate set to the detector. Fuzzy detection clues, including for first contact, are therefore not part of the design (`MPE-CRY-037`, `DEC-020`). Midnight Express recognizes traffic by tags, with two changes from Aztec. The 16-byte Recognition Tag is salted per MPE Envelope, so it gives a relay no stable string to follow. And recognition happens on the Subscriber's device over the whole Shard, so no Bus Node, Store Node or Indexer is told which Recognition Tags anyone tests.

Relays can still read the Recognition Tag. It sits with the salt and the encryption nonce in the clear prefix of the MPE Envelope's Sealed Body, which the encryption authenticates, so a relay can read the prefix but cannot alter it without making the MPE Envelope fail to open. The salt prevents linking by tag and nothing more: a relay can still group MPE Envelopes by Shard, ingress connection, admission data and timing, and the admission stand-in links every MPE Envelope to its member outright. The recognition decision stays open (`DEC-003`), because salted Recognition Tags have not yet been compared with trial decryption under every key; Q1 of Chapter 9 makes that comparison.

One MPE Envelope shows the Subscriber's work. The Publisher computes its Recognition Tag from a fresh salt and a recognition key shared only with the stream's Subscribers (Chapter 8). A Subscriber following three streams recomputes the tag under each of its three keys with that salt; a match makes the MPE Envelope a candidate to open, and no match means it belongs to someone else.

MPS-0005's planned topic filtering is evaluated by the Indexer for a wallet; a Recognition Tag is evaluated only by the Subscriber. In this document's terms, MIP-0002 prefix filters and MPS-0005 topic filtering are reduced-privacy selective profiles (`MPE-PUB-016`, `MPE-PRV-013`), not the default, and the strict profile excludes any infrastructure-side relevance mapping (`MPE-PUB-009`, `MPE-PUB-010`). When a Message carries a contract event that has `@topic` values, those values travel only inside the ciphertext.

The protection is conditional. It holds within a fixed set of received Shards, and only while the Subscriber's subscriptions, requests, retries, errors, repair and receipts do not depend on what it recognized. The transport proves none of this; client behaviour must preserve it (Chapter 4). One dependent action is enough to break it: a Subscriber that repairs gaps only in windows where it recognized something tells the Store Node which windows held its Messages.

The Subscriber's recognition work grows with its keys and the Shard's rate, not with Message size. At the 256-key cap and 10 MPE Envelopes per second it computes 2,560 Recognition Tags per second (256 × 10), each an HMAC that costs two SHA-256 compressions once its state is precomputed for the key, so 5,120 compressions per second (derived). Q1 of Chapter 9 will measure that cost against trial decryption, on phone hardware as well. Light-client reception remains open (`DEC-021`). The proof of concept will measure Subscriber bytes per day against the 56 to 60 MB per day mobile budget that the requirement set records (Q11 in Chapter 9); closing that gap would then need a measured OMR or PIR profile.

## Decentralized publish/subscribe overlays and GossipSub

Publish/subscribe decouples publishers from subscribers through an event service; decentralized versions build that service from the peers themselves. The research overlays route by interest. PolderCast builds a ring per topic plus random shortcuts. It reaches a 100 percent hit ratio without churn and a miss ratio no higher than 0.04 under a Skype churn trace, about a tenth of Scribe's, and delivers no unwanted traffic [@2012-setty-poldercast]. Vitis clusters peers by shared topics and keeps relay traffic to about 4 percent of the total [@2012-rahimian-locality-awareness]. Content-based networks route on subscriber predicates used as addresses [@2011-carzaniga-contentbased].

GossipSub, the libp2p protocol, is topic-based. Each peer keeps a mesh of D peers per GossipSub topic, pushes full messages over the mesh, and gossips message identifiers to other peers, who pull what they lack [@2019-vyzovitis-gossipsub-v01]. At each heartbeat IHAVE advertises recent identifiers to peers outside the mesh, and IWANT requests what a peer lacks, so the gossip repairs what the mesh missed.

Version 1.1 adds locally computed peer scores, extended validators with Accept, Reject and Ignore outcomes, peer exchange, flood publishing of one's own messages, outbound mesh quotas and backoff timers [@2020-gossipsub-v11-spec]. Version 1.2 adds the IDONTWANT control message and advertises `/meshsub/1.2.0`: a peer that receives a message sends its identifier in IDONTWANT to its mesh peers, so those that have not yet forwarded it skip the duplicate. The v1.2 specification is still a Working Draft (revision r2). A v1.2 peer can talk to a v1.1 peer, but that link carries no IDONTWANT.^[libp2p/specs, pubsub/gossipsub/gossipsub-v1.2.md, lifecycle table and section "Protocol Id"; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.]

The overlays hide nothing about interest. PolderCast gossips each node's IP address, port and topic list to its neighbours by design [@2012-setty-poldercast]. GossipSub announces a peer's subscriptions to every connected pubsub peer [@2019-vyzovitis-gossipsub-v01], and its control traffic adds more: IHAVE and IWANT show which message identifiers a peer holds or lacks, and IDONTWANT, which a peer may send before it validates a message, shows when it received each one. A security review of GossipSub adds that a neighbour can estimate its hop distance from a publisher by reaction time, and that GossipSub "does not try to obscure this information" [@2020-leastauthority-gossipsub-audit, p. 7]. Cryptographic publish/subscribe schemes hide content and the meaning of subscriptions from a broker, but the broker still performs the match. They also rely on a fully trusted key authority that must not collude with the broker [@2014-yang-privatepubsubcloud], or they hide the payload only [@2017-polyakov-prepubsub].

In a *Sybil* attack one party runs many identities; in an *eclipse* attack the adversary makes every connection of a victim lead to its own peers. GossipSub's authors evaluated D = 8, D_low = 6 and D_high = 12 with a 1-second heartbeat, the profile Midnight Express adopts. In one experiment, 1,000 honest nodes on a warm network faced 4,000 Sybils at a 20:1 connection ratio, and a network-wide eclipse attempt still left a p99 propagation latency of 141 ms [@2020-vyzovitis-gossipsub]. That figure describes that experiment; it is not a latency guarantee. The version 1.1 evaluation found no case with a p99 delay above 1.6 seconds at Sybil-to-honest connection ratios up to 40:1 [@2020-vyzovitis-gossipsub-v11-evaluation, p. 5]. In a publisher-targeted eclipse at 40:1, version 1.0 delivered about 71 percent of messages and version 1.1 delivered all of them [@2020-vyzovitis-gossipsub-v11-evaluation, sec. 9.5].

Eclipse is cheap elsewhere too: 4,600 bots eclipse a Bitcoin node with at least 85 percent probability [@2015-heilman-eclipse], and Kademlia-based eclipse of IPFS nodes cost about 0.031 EUR per hour per victim before fixes [@2022-prunster-ipfseclipse]. The scores are not a safe default. Formal analysis found configurations in which a peer that never forwards keeps a positive score, Ethereum's among them [@2022-kumar-gossipsub-formal].

Source anonymity in gossip has firm limits. Under a spy-based adversary, no spreading protocol detects the source with probability below the spy fraction p, and plain diffusion approaches certainty as degree grows [@2015-fanti-hidingrumor]. Guerraoui et al. show that if f nodes are curious, no gossip protocol achieves ε-differential privacy for the source with ε < ln(f − 1), and on poorly connected graphs none achieves any [@2023-guerraoui-inherent-anonymity-gossiping]. Source privacy at level ε means that moving the source to any other node changes the probability of anything the curious nodes observe by at most a factor e^ε. For a coalition of 20 relays, no protocol can keep that factor below 19 (derived: ε ≥ ln 19, so e^ε ≥ 19).

On Bitcoin, Biryukov et al. linked transactions to sender IP addresses for 11 percent of transactions with no more than 50 connections per server, and up to 60 percent with a slight denial of service, for under 1,500 EUR a month [@2014-biryukov-deanonymisation].

Dandelion is one remedy. It passes a new message along a random line of single hops, the *stem*, before diffusing it, so the node where diffusion starts is usually not the source. It bounds recall at p and precision near p² [@2017-venkatakrishnan-dandelion]. Dandelion++ repairs attacks on the line graph using a 4-regular anonymity graph that it re-randomizes about every ten minutes [@2018-fanti-dandelionpp]. Both proofs assume a graph the adversary does not know. Without that assumption little remains: in simulated Ethereum-like topologies, an adversary with 10 percent of nodes "can be almost sure" of the source under plain broadcast, and Dandelion improves the source's rank by about half a place [@2023-beres-ethp2psim].

**Waku.** Waku v2 is the closest deployed system to Midnight Express, because its relay is GossipSub [@2020-vac-waku2-relay-spec]. It strips the sender fields from messages (`StrictNoSign`) and states its limits: a publisher's direct peers "might be able to figure out which Messages belong to that publisher", and global observers are out of scope. Its history service requires the querier to reveal its content filters, with no anonymity for historical queries [@2020-vac-waku2-store-spec], and its light subscription mode discloses topics to the serving node [@2020-vac-waku2-filter-spec].

Admission in Waku uses rate-limiting nullifiers (RLN). A member may publish one message per epoch, and a second message reveals the member's secret through Shamir shares [@2021-vac-waku2-rln-relay-spec]. The mechanism is a line. The member's secret a_0 and the epoch fix a slope a_1, and each message reveals one point on y = a_0 + a_1·x, with x a hash of the message [@2022-vac-rln-v1-spec]. One point says nothing about a_0; two points from one epoch fix the line, and its value at x = 0 is the secret. Proof generation takes 85.7 ms on an M1 and 766.8 ms on a Raspberry Pi 4, verification 2.7 to 18.7 ms, and a 1,000-node simulation delivered 2 KB messages in 497 ms on average, with a p95 of 597 ms [@2024-revuelta-waku-latency]. Waku's authors name RLN's weakness: one attacker can buy k memberships and send k messages per epoch [@2022-taheri-waku-rln-relay, p. 6].

GossipSub supplies what Midnight Express needs from a transport: resilience to Sybil and eclipse attacks, measured in its authors' experiments at the chosen mesh profile; extended validation, whose Accept, Reject and Ignore outcomes are the hook for admission; application-defined message identifiers, so that every Bus Node can hash the complete MPE Envelope; and, since version 1.2, IDONTWANT. Without IDONTWANT, a Bus Node with eight mesh peers can receive one class-3 MPE Envelope of 16,904 B from each of them, eight copies for one Envelope. IDONTWANT replaces each later copy with a notice naming a 32-byte identifier, but only for peers that receive the notice before they send, so the saving depends on message size and link timing. Q5 of Chapter 9 states it as a hypothesis with a pass condition: at least 30 percent less class-3 egress, net of the notices.

The transport profile (Chapter 8) requires v1.2 behaviour: the v1.1 scoring and mesh rules plus IDONTWANT. GossipSub topics for Shards and request-response protocols use names of the form `/mpe/<g>/1`, where g is the first eight bytes of the genesis hash in lowercase hexadecimal. The GossipSub protocol identifier, the string two peers must agree on before any GossipSub traffic flows, is the exception. The profile requires every mesh link to negotiate the standard `/meshsub/1.2.0`, pinned, because the libraries also offer v1.3 and older versions and a default offer list does not guarantee v1.2.

The rust-libp2p source gives a second reason to pin the standard identifier. Stock rust-libp2p gossipsub 0.50 runs v1.2 behaviour, IDONTWANT included, only under `/meshsub/1.2.0`. Its `protocol_id` configuration method selects the behaviour through a `Version` argument that accepts only `V1_0` or `V1_1`, so under a custom identifier the library runs v1.0 or v1.1 behaviour whatever the string says.^[libp2p/rust-libp2p, protocols/gossipsub/src/config.rs, enum `Version` and `ConfigBuilder::protocol_id`; libp2p-gossipsub 0.50.0 and 0.51.0 source, accessed 1 October 2026.] A custom identifier with v1.2 behaviour would need a patched copy of the library. Peers link only over an identifier both offer, so a mesh that offered only the custom identifier would also exclude every peer that offers only `/meshsub/1.2.0`. The standard identifier gets v1.2 behaviour from the stock library and keeps the mesh open to standard peers.

D_out is the number of mesh peers a node must have dialled itself, which keeps an attacker who floods it with inbound connections from filling its whole mesh. The chosen tuple (D, D_low, D_high, D_out) = (8, 6, 12, 4) meets the v1.1 constraints D_out < D_low and D_out ≤ D/2, but the Go implementation's validator demands D_out < D/2 and rejects it.^[libp2p/go-libp2p-pubsub, gossipsub.go, function GossipSubParams.validate; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] With D = 8, D/2 = 4, and D_out = 4 satisfies 4 ≤ 4 but not 4 < 4.

GossipSub supplies no privacy, so privacy must come from what the overlay carries, and four limits apply to it. The first is the GossipSub topic name. A name of the form `/mpe/<g>/1/shard/<i>` discloses the network prefix, the protocol's major version and the Shard. With one Shard at launch it does not separate Subscribers by Shard, but it reveals participation, and the connection reveals endpoint identity, online schedule and traffic.

The second concerns sizes and recognition. Four fixed size classes hide payload length within a class, while the class itself, message timing, volume and the complete transport transcript (framing, batching and control messages) stay visible. The Recognition Tag is readable but salted, and interest privacy holds only under the conditions of the previous section.

The third is publish ingress, which is a one-hop stem with no anonymity claim (`DEC-023`). The client hands its MPE Envelope to one Bus Node, and the Dandelion++ bound assumes a random graph, which a GossipSub mesh is not. Bus Nodes publish under StrictNoSign, as Waku does. That removes the pubsub author stamp but not the authenticated libp2p identity and address of the connection, so the ingress Bus Node sees the submitting endpoint directly. Disabling flood publishing reduces egress; it does not hide the origin. The rust-libp2p 0.50 decoder also accepts a message that carries only the `key` field (the author's public key in signed GossipSub), which StrictNoSign forbids, and a later release adds the check.^[libp2p/rust-libp2p, protocols/gossipsub/CHANGELOG.md, entry adding rejection of the `key` field in `ValidationMode::Anonymous`; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] A Bus Node built on 0.50 is therefore not fully StrictNoSign until it adds the check itself or moves to the release that has it.

The fourth is the relay set, which is permissioned at launch (`DEC-006`). Scoring contributes to attack resistance, but it neither limits identity creation nor guarantees Sybil resistance on its own. Outbound quotas resist capture through inbound connections only while honest dial sources exist, and poisoned discovery remains a risk.

The Waku record adds two cautions. First, history queries leak whatever the client filters on, so back-fill in this design is by Shard and time window only, and repair retrieves complete windows whatever the Subscriber recognized.

Second, per-membership quotas are local decisions. A membership holds *credits* in each 60-second admission window, 64, 16, 4 and 1 for size classes 0 to 3, and each MPE Envelope spends one credit of its class. Each Bus Node checks a credit only against the admission nullifiers it has itself accepted. A membership that submits two different MPE Envelopes under its single class-3 credit to two ingress Bus Nodes at once therefore has both accepted, one at each, and the conflict appears only when the copies meet; with k ingress Bus Nodes the same credit can be accepted k times. No network-wide admission count follows from a connected mesh. Q9 of Chapter 9 will measure how many conflicting MPE Envelopes reach Subscribers before the evidence spreads.

In RLN, two shares from one allowance expose the secret and justify removal. An exploratory Bus Registry contract written in Compact for this design performs that check. There the slope of a member's line is derived from the member's secret, the admission window, the size class and the credit, so two MPE Envelopes that spend one credit give two points on one line. Its revocation circuit accepts two such shares with distinct envelope identifiers, plus the member's Merkle path to the published membership root. It removes the member and records the identity commitment. The circuit was compiled and costed, not run on a network. Like Waku's, it detects a breach after the fact and does not prevent it. Its RLN relation uses Compact's `transientHash`, which carries no stability guarantee across upgrades, and its slope omits the network and the Bus Registry, so one secret used on two networks or with two Bus Registries would give two points on one line and expose the secret. The compiled circuit is therefore an exploratory placeholder. Version 1 fixes the relation's inputs as `a1 = H("mpe/v1/rln/a1", N, R, a0, window, class, i)`, with `N` the genesis hash and `R` the Bus Registry contract address, under a hash `H` that Midnight documents as stable (Chapter 8).

## Deployed messengers

Messaging systems that have shipped to users are where content privacy is mature and metadata privacy is weakest.

**Signal.** Signal's protocol has been proved secure as a key exchange; metadata is outside that analysis [@2016-cohngordon-signal]. Its asynchronous setup stores prekey bundles, the public keys a user leaves on a server so that others can open a session while the user is offline, and the server learns who requests whose bundle [@2016-signal-x3dh-spec]. *Sealed sender* hides the sender from Signal's server, but the recipient remains visible and delivery receipts are automatic. Martiny et al. show that Signal "could link sealed sender users in as few as 5 messages". They measured a median of 1,480 ms between a message and its receipt, and receipts cannot be turned off [@2021-martiny-sealed-sender, pp. 1-3]. The receipt is itself a sealed message whose visible recipient is the original sender, so a server that repeatedly sees a message for Bob followed about a second and a half later by one for Alice links the two without breaking any encryption.

Silent receipts triggered by reactions, edits and deletions then turned out to be a covert ping. Round-trip times of about 350 ms for an app in the foreground, and above one second with the screen off, expose a target's device state without any notification [@2024-gegenhuber-careless-whisper].

**Matrix.** Matrix replicates every room event, with its sender and timestamp, to every homeserver with a member in the room [@2015-matrix-federation-api]. A malicious homeserver can break its confidentiality and authentication guarantees, because it controls the room's member and device lists [@2023-albrecht-matrix].

**Systems built to reduce metadata.** Each of these hides something from its servers and leaves something else in view.

- SimpleX uses unidirectional queues with no user identities and pads every transport block to a fixed 16,384 bytes. The router still sees each queue's timing and volume [@2021-simplex-smp-spec].
- Session stores messages on swarms of 5 to 10 staked nodes, chosen by the recipient's identifier and reached through three-hop onion requests. The swarm learns the recipient identifier, message count and timing, and the paper concedes that a global observer makes traffic analysis "trivial" [@2020-jefferys-session, pp. 4-10].
- Cwtch makes every group member download every message a relay server holds, and calls this "equivalent to the naive PIR design" [@2018-lewis-cwtch, p. 10].
- Briar recognizes streams by a 16-byte pseudo-random tag but disclaims anonymity outright [@2016-briar-btp].
- Secure Scuttlebutt posts private messages on a public signed feed, which hides recipients and content but shows the sender, time and size [@2017-ssb-protocol-guide].

**Nostr.** Nostr's direct messages wrap a sealed message in an outer wrapper signed by a one-time key, with timestamps randomized up to two days into the past [@nostr-nip-17; @nostr-nip-59]. The outer wrapper still tags the recipient's public key for the relay, and the specification warns that groups larger than about 10 need another scheme. A measurement of 712 relays found each post replicated across 34.6 relays on average, 98.2 percent of retrievals redundant, and 95 percent of free relays unable to cover their costs [@2024-wei-nostr-empirical, pp. 2-3].

Four lessons transfer to Midnight Express. SimpleX's fixed 16,384-byte blocks and this design's largest size class reach the same answer, that length must not vary, and SimpleX shows the limit of that answer: timing and volume stay visible. The sealed-sender attack shows that a server which knows the recipient, as Session's swarms and Nostr's relays do, defeats any sender protection after a handful of exchanges; whole-Shard reception exists to avoid that.

The third lesson is that a receipt or reaction sent automatically turns a private channel into an oracle. Delivery receipts in this design are therefore not automatic, and Consumers that react on chain are treated as observable (Chapter 4), because a contract reaction is a public transaction. The exploratory consumer circuit written in Compact for this design (compiled, not run) discloses a consumption nullifier, which stops a Message from being consumed twice, together with the action and a coarse expiry bound chosen by the prover, and the transaction's timing is visible to every chain observer.

The fourth lesson is the operator economics. Nostr's relays are mostly unfunded, and deployed Waku registers memberships for an on-chain transaction fee without the slashable deposit of the original RLN proposal [@2024-revuelta-waku-latency, pp. 5-6]. A private event bus without a source of funding inherits the same decay, so the requirement set funds launch Bus Operators off-protocol (`DEC-001`). Midnight offers no on-chain path to pay them in DUST. DUST is non-transferable and only pays transaction fees, so a contract can neither receive it nor observe a price paid in it. A Bus Registry price beyond the network fee would have to be denominated in NIGHT, Midnight's native token, or in another token. Bonds, where the requirement set uses them, are Bus Registry contract balances and have no relation to NIGHT staking.

## Ledger-based carriers and stealth addressing

The final family uses the chain itself as the carrier. The ledger is a public broadcast medium that everyone already replicates, so a sealed message on it gains Bitmessage's recipient hiding at no extra network cost, with ordering and finality besides.

CryptoNote's stealth addresses send each payment to a one-time key derived from the recipient's public keys and a random sender value, and the recipient "checks every passing transaction" to find its own [@2013-vansaberhagen-cryptonote, p. 7]. In Zcash every shielded output carries a 512-byte memo inside a 580-byte note ciphertext [@2016-hopwood-zcash-protocol-spec, pp. 16, 134]. Zerocash's authors noted that scanning in the worst case means "a trial decryption of every ciphertext", and that implementations may notify recipients out of band instead [@2014-bensasson-zerocash, p. 28].

On Bitcoin, BIP47 announces a payment code through an 80-byte OP_RETURN notification, which leaks through its change outputs [@2015-bip47-payment-codes]. Silent Payments avoid notification: the receiver computes a key exchange against the inputs of every eligible transaction, which costs light clients about 7 to 12 kB per block of tweak data [@2023-bip352-silent-payments]. On Ethereum, ERC-5564 emits an `Announcement` log whose first metadata byte is a view tag, which cuts parsing about sixfold and reduces the security margin from 128 to 124 bits [@2022-erc5564-stealth]. A measured implementation parsed 80,000 announcements in 4.89 seconds with view tags and in 37.56 seconds without, at about 35,492 gas per announcement [@2023-wahrstatter-basesap, pp. 6-8]. Cardano carries messages in transaction metadata under label 674, as plaintext strings of at most 64 bytes [@2021-cip20-tx-message]. Its encryption extension defaults to a fixed passphrase, so it obscures the text without protecting it [@2022-cip83-encrypted-metadata].

Every recipient scans every candidate output, and every message pays a transaction fee and occupies block space. The anonymity set is whatever usage turns out to be. Kappos et al. found that only 0.3 percent of Zcash transactions (6,934 of 2,242,847) were fully shielded, and linked 65.6 percent of the value leaving the shielded pool to known deposits [@2018-kappos-zcash-anonymity, pp. 2-4].

Announcements and registries are public by design. A sender's funding transaction can link a stealth payment to its source [@2022-erc5564-stealth], and announcement spam costs scanners work that no fee covers. XMTP keeps identity updates on its own chain forever but message bodies off it [@2024-xmtp-xip49-decentralized-backend]: the ledger orders and authorizes, and the bodies travel elsewhere.

Midnight already contains this family. Zswap, Midnight's shielded-coin scheme, gives each output a ciphertext to the recipient key, bound into the proof [@2024-midnight-zswap-spec]. In the ledger-9 generation that the node pins, the ciphertext is one curve point plus six field elements, enough for coin data and nothing else.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, zswap/src/structure.rs, constant COIN_CIPHERTEXT_LEN; tag ledger-9.1.0.0-rc.5, accessed 1 October 2026.] The specification adds that an output owned by a contract carries no ciphertext at all. Wallets find their coins through the Indexer, the service that reads chain data and serves it to wallets and DApps [@midnight-mps-0028]: they subscribe to its global stream of Zswap ledger events and trial-decrypt each one locally, which is the CryptoNote pattern.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-indexer, indexer-api/graphql/schema-v4.graphql, subscription zswapLedgerEvents; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.]

The Foundation's token proposals work around the gap in opposite ways. MIP-0011 tells callers to deliver the information for coins a contract mints "out of band", because wallets cannot find those coins by scanning [@midnight-mip-0011]. For custody, MIP-0012 keeps an append-only inbox of fixed 192-byte encrypted entries in contract state, because discovery and total-loss recovery must work "from chain data alone" [@midnight-mip-0012]. The first is a need an off-chain channel can serve; the second is a case where Midnight Express must not be offered as a substitute.

Contract events, defined by MIP-0002, are public [@midnight-mip-0002]. The `emit` mechanism carries a 32-byte name and a 256-byte `Misc` payload whose fields must all be disclosed, and the virtual machine silently drops events above 1 KiB (Chapter 2). Events arrive with ledger 9; mainnet ran ledger 8.0 as of 4 August 2026, and MIP-0002 is live on Stagenet. MIP-0019 (Proposed) groups several `Misc` parts with one name, from one transaction, one physical intent (a sealed group of contract calls inside the included transaction, applied as a unit) and one execution phase, into a multipart package. It also states the limit of encrypting such payloads: "Application encryption may hide payload plaintext but does not hide that metadata or retract disclosed data" [@midnight-mip-0019].

MPS-0005 (Proposed) plans the Foundation's private events as a Phase 2 extension of the contract event: fields marked `@private` are encrypted with `encrypt_for` for chosen recipients and bound to the transaction by a `proven` commitment that the proof checks [@midnight-mps-0005]. Its Part 2 text is planned and unpublished. A private event in that sense is produced by contract execution and authenticated to the contract and entry point that emitted it [@midnight-mps-0043]. A Message is produced by a Publisher's client, authenticated by the Publisher's signature, and committed only through an Anchor: an authorized anchorer's commitment, posted on chain, to the Envelope Identifiers it accepted in one 60-second window. Midnight Express is therefore a complement to on-chain private events, not an alternative, and it does not replace MPS-0005 Part 2.

Under stated conditions it is also a transport for them. A *carried event* is a Message whose payload is the unchanged bytes of a contract event, public or private, together with the transaction hash and the event's position. A Consumer checks it against the transcript, the public record of on-chain operations that the transaction's proof binds. Four of the seven conditions of Chapter 4 bear on the carrier: the bytes must exist off chain when the transaction is built; the chain must keep the event bound to its transaction; an event of up to 1 KiB plus its references must fit a size class; and Consumers must treat it as unconfirmed until the transaction is finalized and the bytes match the transcript. A public event is bound already, since MIP-0002 records all its fields in the transaction, so carrying one waits on no unpublished decision, only on ledger 9. A private event stays bound either through its ciphertext in the transcript or through a `proven` commitment to it. Which of the two Part 2 allows is unknown, so carried private events wait on that text. Either way the event pays its fee once, on chain, and its authority stays with the transaction; Midnight Express adds early delivery and interest-hiding.

MPS-0043 assigns "choosing how the data travels" for confidential data to a pending problem statement, Confidential Contract Notifications (`mps-xxxx`, PR #271) [@midnight-mps-0043], whose content is unknown here. The problem statement at the end of Chapter 4 covers the same delivery, and whether it is that statement, a companion or a replacement is for the Foundation to settle.

Midnight Express's own carrier of this kind is the *ledger lane*: a Message's body is published on chain as `Misc` contract events, which reach Subscribers through the Indexer without any Bus Operator's cooperation (Option 1 in Chapter 6). On the ledger lane a Message of class c travels as 4^c `Misc` parts of 256 bytes, because each class body is four times the one below: classes 0, 1 and 2 use 1, 4 and 16 parts, and class 3 would need 64 and has no ledger lane. Each part counts as 288 bytes of struct data, its 32-byte name plus its 256-byte payload. Two per-block limits could bind. One is `bytes_written`, 50,000 B of net state growth per block shared by every contract. The other is `block_usage`, the total transaction bytes in a block: `block_usage` is 1,000,000 B in the Midnight node's network configuration files and 200,000 B in the ledger's initial limits, and which value a live network runs is unknown.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-node, res/mainnet/ledger-parameters-config.json, `block_limits`; midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, ledger/src/structure.rs, constant `INITIAL_LIMITS`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] MIP-0002 and the ledger-9 cost code disagree on which limit an event draws on.

MIP-0002 sizes event capacity against `bytes_written`, with about 40 bytes of overhead per event [@midnight-mip-0002]. Each part then costs about 328 B (288 + 40), a class-1 Message about 1,312 B and a class-2 Message about 5,248 B. The whole chain carries about 38 class-1 Messages per block (50,000 / 1,312), about 6.3 per second (3.5 if `block_usage` is 200,000 B), or about 9 class-2 Messages per block (50,000 / 5,248), about 1.5 per second, and only if no other contract writes anything. One Shard at 10 Messages per second needs 60 per 6-second block, which at class 1 is 78,720 B of `bytes_written` per block (60 × 1,312), more than the whole chain's budget. Under MIP-0002's sizing the ledger cannot carry one Shard at launch load.

The ledger-9 cost code books a log differently.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, onchain-vm/src/vm.rs, opcode `Log`, and base-crypto/src/cost_model.rs, conversion to net bytes written; tag ledger-9.1.0.0-rc.4, accessed 1 October 2026.] The code charges a logged event as both written and deleted, and the block limit applies to net written bytes, so under the code an event adds nothing to `bytes_written`; its bytes count as churn, bytes that pass through state without staying, against a separate per-block limit that one Shard does not approach, and `block_usage` binds. With proofs counted, one Shard of class-1 Messages takes 60 transactions of about 9,248 B per block, about 554,880 B (derived: 60 × 9,248): about 2.8 times a 200,000 B limit and about 55 percent of a 1,000,000 B one, against the 10 percent share of block capacity that the requirement set allows the ledger lane (P-ECO-8). Which reading governs a live ledger-9 network, and which `block_usage` value is live, is unmeasured (`MPE-PRF-040`). Planning uses the lowest figure for each class: about 4.0 class-0, 3.5 class-1 and 1.5 class-2 Messages per second for the whole chain with no other writers, and 0.40, 0.35 and 0.15 inside the P-ECO-8 share.

The costs that follow hold under either reading. Every ledger-lane Message is part of a transaction that pays DUST. Derived from the ledger-9 cost model at genesis prices, one class-0 Message alone costs about 0.129 DUST, or about 111,000 DUST per day at 10 per second (0.129 × 10 × 86,400); sixteen parts cost about 0.188 DUST in one call and 0.866 DUST in sixteen separate calls.

Proving adds a second cost, counted in circuit rows: a circuit of size k has room for 2^k rows. Compact packs bytes into field elements, so emitting a payload that is not a constant means unpacking it byte by byte. A constant 256-byte payload costs 23 rows, and a 256-byte circuit argument, which is what a ledger-lane part is, costs 166,241. That lies between 2^17 = 131,072 and 2^18 = 262,144, so one part needs k = 18. Four parts in one call are k = 20, and sixteen parts about k = 22. The k = 18 proving keys alone are 67 to 76 MB.

A Midnight transaction has a guaranteed segment, whose effects hold whenever the transaction is included, and a fallible segment, which can fail on its own and then discards its events; MIP-0019 calls each an execution phase. Large transcripts exceed the guaranteed segment's time budget, so ledger-lane parts share the fallible segment of one intent, as MIP-0019 allows. A ledger-lane Message then counts as published only once its transaction is included and that segment has succeeded. If the segment fails, the Message is lost and the fee is still paid.

The design therefore follows XMTP's split. The Bus Registry contract holds membership, quota roots and one Anchor per 60-second window in its state, and Message bodies never enter contract state; the Anchor's copy as a `Misc` event named `mip-xxxx:anchor[v1]` is a notification, because events are not consensus state. Exploratory versions of the contracts specified in Chapter 8 were written in Compact and compiled with Compact 0.35.0 (language 0.27.0) against a ledger-9 release candidate (`emit` requires ledger 9 and toolchain 0.33 or later). They were costed, not run on a network, and their fees are derived from the cost model, not measured. They are exploratory code, and the design does not depend on them being final. A registration is about 8.7 KB and 0.246 DUST (0.308 with the wallet's margin). An Anchor post costs 0.195 DUST (0.244 with margin), about 280 DUST per day at one Anchor per 60 s (1,440 × 0.195), and needs a k = 18 proof. The 0.195 DUST assumes no net state growth; each retained Anchor-tree root adds about 32 B, about 0.006 DUST per Anchor or about 9 DUST per day (derived: 32 × 0.0002; 1,440 × 0.0064), until a reset rule bounds the tree. Every circuit's proof is at most 4,368 B and its verifying key at most 2,119 B, so the verifier's cost stays flat while the prover's grows.

Compiling the contracts also exposes the limits of a ledger carrier on Midnight. A Midnight transaction has no signer, so authority is a hash preimage proved in circuit. A contract can compare block time but not read it, so Bus Nodes, not the contract, enforce the 3,600 s root window off chain. A consumer circuit that proves only Anchor inclusion has no binding between its consumption nullifier and the Message; that binding needs a publisher signature checked in circuit, and that circuit has not been written. A signature proves who authorized an instruction, not that the overlay admitted or anchored the Message; proving that needs a binding between the signed statement and the anchored Envelope that version 1 does not specify. Finality bounds the ledger lane's latency from below: with 6-second blocks and finality after about 3 blocks (Chapter 2), a ledger-lane Message is final no sooner than about 18 s (3 × 6 s) after its transaction is included, before the wait for a block and the proving time are added. Q16 of Chapter 9 sets its pass condition at a p95 of 60 s (Option 1 in Chapter 6).

From the stealth-address record the design takes the 1-byte view tag, whose effect the salted 16-byte Recognition Tag reproduces off chain, and Kappos's warning that a privacy feature used by few hides little. With one Shard at launch, a Subscriber hides among everyone else receiving that Shard, and how large that crowd becomes depends on adoption, not on the design.

The rows bear out the trilemma. Every family that hides the recipient makes someone handle traffic that is not theirs; every family that hides the sender from a global observer adds cover traffic, delay or trusted servers. Only gossip runs at 10 MPE Envelopes per second on ordinary hardware without trusted servers. The ledger orders and authorizes well, but under MIP-0002's `bytes_written` sizing the whole chain carries about 6.3 class-1 Messages per second (3.5 if `block_usage` is 200,000 B), fewer than one Shard needs, and under either reading each Message pays DUST and a proof. Neither gossip nor the ledger hides anything by itself. Midnight Express combines them: gossip carries sealed, fixed-size MPE Envelopes, the Subscriber pays Bitmessage's cost of receiving everything, bounded by the Shard, and the ledger provides admission, ordering and a slow ledger lane. The privacy this delivers is the limited set defined in Chapter 4.

Two obstacles remain open. Light-client reception (`DEC-021`) needs a measured OMR or PIR profile to close the gap between the 1.86 GB per day derived for whole-Shard reception at the baseline mix and a mobile budget of 56 to 60 MB per day, a factor of about 31 (1,865 MB / 60 MB, derived). Sender anonymity against a global observer is out of reach at β = 0 and is reached only by the mixnet and PIR lineage (Option 4 in Chapter 6). For each, the open question is which scheme, measured at 10 MPE Envelopes per second, buys the missing property at a cost a Midnight client can carry.
