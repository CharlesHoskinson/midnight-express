# Architecture options for Midnight {#ch6}

Chapter 5 placed the prior art by what it hides and what it costs. Those families give five architectures for delivering Confidential Messages on Midnight, and all five are assessed here against one set of criteria. The criteria are fixed before the detailed requirements of Chapter 7, so that no option is measured against rules written to fit it.

## What the options carry

A Message is an application message that a Publisher seals and delivers. Sealing means that the Publisher encrypts the Message for its recipients and signs it: a party without the recipients' keys cannot read it, and a party without the Publisher's signing key cannot change it undetected. A Confidential Message is a Message that holds the four properties of Chapter 4: content confidentiality, sealed labels, conditional interest privacy and no silent change. None of the options redefines the Foundation's terms. A Midnight event is a contract event, emitted by a Compact circuit with `emit` and recorded in the transaction [@midnight-mip-0002; @midnight-coip-0003]. A private event is a contract event some of whose fields are encrypted for chosen recipients and bound to the transaction by a commitment the proof checks. MPS-0005 plans private events as its Phase 2 and scopes them in a Part 2 whose text is unpublished [@midnight-mps-0005]. Of the event documents cited here, only MIP-0002 is Accepted. MIP-0018, MIP-0019, MPS-0005, MPS-0007 and MPS-0043 are Proposed, and CoIP-0003 has Draft status.

An MPE Envelope, or Envelope (Chapter 8), is the sealed, fixed-size container of one Message, in one of four size classes numbered 0 to 3. A Shard is a partition of the Envelope traffic whose Subscribers each receive all of it; there is one Shard at launch. An Anchor is a ledger record, posted once per 60-second window, that commits to the Envelopes seen in that window. A Subscriber receives Envelopes from a Shard, and a Consumer acts on a Message (Chapter 3). For a contract the two differ, because a contract cannot receive anything by itself.

A Confidential Message is not a contract event. No contract executes it; its Publisher's signature authenticates it, and an Anchor commits to it. Nothing carried off the chain can stand in for "the contract emitted this field", so no option below is an alternative to on-chain private events, and none replaces MPS-0005 Part 2. An escrow contract's release event is backed by a proof the chain has verified. A Message from the seller saying that the escrow has released is the seller's signed statement: a reader can check it against the chain, but no contract stands behind it.

The options serve a need the Foundation's own documents state. MIP-0011 asks callers to deliver coin information "out of band" [@midnight-mip-0011], that is, by some channel other than the transaction itself. MPS-0025 asks for encrypted transfer context that may be provided out of band [@midnight-mps-0025]. MIP-0005 records that offer files carry no messages [@midnight-mip-0005], and MIP-0006 already uses an off-chain shared namespace for offers [@midnight-mip-0006]. MPS-0043 assigns "choosing how the data travels" to a pending problem statement, Confidential Contract Notifications (`mps-xxxx`), whose content is unknown at the time of writing [@midnight-mps-0043]. Custody is the counter-case. MIP-0012 rejects out-of-band delivery for its inbox, because discovery and total-loss recovery must work from chain data alone [@midnight-mip-0012]. Where that property is required, an off-chain carrier is the wrong tool.

A carried event is a Confidential Message whose payload is the unchanged bytes of a contract event, public or private, with the transaction hash and position that let a Consumer check it against the chain. Once the transaction is finalized, the Consumer compares the carried bytes with the transcript, the public record of the transaction's ledger operations, and accepts or discards the copy. An option that can carry such events complements on-chain events without touching their authority, and it transports them when five conditions hold:

- the client has the event's bytes, exactly as the ledger will record them, when the transaction is built;
- the chain keeps the binding: the ciphertext or a commitment to it stays in the transcript;
- the event, its transaction hash and its position fit one size class;
- the recipients can recognise it;
- Consumers treat it as unconfirmed until the transaction is finalized and the bytes match the transcript.

## The criteria and where they come from

The criteria have two sources. The first is the privacy definition of Chapter 4: its named properties and its five adversary classes (a single relay, the Indexer, a chain observer, a colluding minority of relays, and a global passive observer). The second is the ten decision areas that organize the requirement set: Message format (D1), the definition of private (D2), the publish and subscribe model (D3), a sustainable model (D4), performance (D5), storage (D6), infrastructure actors (D7), the network tether (D8), threats (D9), and the build and verification plan (D10). Interest, in the criteria, means which Messages a Subscriber is waiting for. An Indexer that learns that one wallet asks for one particular stream has learned that wallet's interest, even if it can read nothing the stream carries.

- **Privacy properties delivered** (Chapter 4; D2, D9). Which adversaries are denied the content of a Message and the interest of a Subscriber. Which properties remain out of reach. Whether the option's own infrastructure becomes a new observer.
- **Fit with Midnight today** (D1, D8). What the option needs from the node, the ledger, the Indexer and Compact as they exist in code, and in which generation of that code. Whether it follows the Foundation's event model (MIP-0002, MIP-0019) and leaves on-chain private events to the chain.
- **Performance** (D5). Latency from publication to recognition, sustained throughput per Shard, and the per-node bandwidth the option needs to reach the launch load of 10 Envelopes per second and 64 KiB per second per Shard.
- **Storage** (D6). What must be kept, by whom, for how long, and whether it can expire.
- **Operator burden and incentives** (D4, D7). Who has to run new software, who pays them, and in what unit.
- **Upgrade path** (D3, D10). Whether the option can later gain stronger properties without being replaced, and whether it can be built and measured in stages.
- **Risk** (D9, D10). What would make the option fail, how probable that failure is on current evidence, and how visible it would be.

Every option is held to the same limited privacy claim: content confidentiality and interest hiding against infrastructure, with no claim against a global observer and no timing or relationship privacy at launch. An option that offers more is credited for it. An option that offers less fails this criterion whatever its other merits.

## The five options

The five options differ in where they put the Message bodies and which parties carry them (Figure 6.1).

{{fig:6-1}}

The first difference is how much each option leans on the Midnight ledger, on which every option except Option 5 depends. Option 1 puts everything there. Option 2 puts membership, quota roots and Anchors there and carries bodies elsewhere, and Options 3 and 4 keep that ledger role and change the carrier. Admission is the check a relay makes before it carries an Envelope: that the Envelope comes from a registered membership which has not yet spent its budget for the current 60-second admission window. A quota root is the ledger's commitment to the memberships and their budgets, so that every relay checks admission against the same record while the Envelopes never pass through the ledger. Option 5 moves admission to another chain, leaving Midnight's ledger at most the Anchors carried back to it. The second difference is who operates the carrier. Options 1 and 3 reuse parties Midnight already has. Option 2 creates a new network of operators, and Option 5 borrows one. Option 4 depends on a mix or retrieval network that Midnight does not have.

## Option 1: Ledger and Indexer only

**Mechanism.** A Publisher seals the Message and publishes the ciphertext from a shared lane contract through Compact's `emit`, as one or more `Misc` events. Each `Misc` event carries a 32-byte name and a 256-byte payload; its struct data is 288 bytes, which is not its serialized size. Parts are named under the Foundation's event-name-as-version convention, `mip-xxxx:envelope[v1]`, padded with NUL bytes to 32 bytes [@midnight-mip-0018]. Subscribers follow the contract's event stream through the Indexer, the service that reads chain data and serves it to wallets and DApps [@midnight-mps-0028], using its `contractEvents` subscription, and trial-decrypt every entry locally: the device tries its keys on each ciphertext and keeps only an entry that opens. Wallets already find shielded coins this way, by testing a global stream of Zswap events against their own keys. Publishers pay the ordinary transaction fee in DUST, the non-transferable resource that holding NIGHT generates; validators order the events, and the Indexer serves them.

**The case for it.** The ledger already provides ordering, finality, a fee market and a carrier that every node replicates. A Midnight transaction has no signer at the Substrate layer, and its DUST spend is shielded, so a chain observer sees a fee value but not who paid it. The Indexer's contract event filter requires a contract address.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-indexer, indexer-api/graphql/schema-v4.graphql, input `ContractEventFilter`, field `contractAddress`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] If every Subscriber follows the same lane contract, the Indexer learns only that a client follows that contract, which every honest Subscriber does. With a thousand such Subscribers and one Message meant for one of them, all thousand receive the same rows, and nothing the Indexer sees marks the recipient. Content confidentiality and interest hiding against the Indexer and a chain observer then come from sealing and full-stream reading alone, with no relay to trust and no new attack surface. The anonymity results usually cited for gossip (Dandelion++, for example) assume a random anonymity graph that the adversary does not know [@2018-fanti-dandelionpp], and gossip in general cannot give a source strong differential privacy against more than a few curious nodes [@2023-guerraoui-inherent-anonymity-gossiping]. On that reading an overlay adds operators and code without buying publisher anonymity, and the ledger-only design is the smallest one that delivers the claimed properties.

**Privacy delivered.** Against a single relay there is nothing to protect, because there are no relays. Against the Indexer and a chain observer the option delivers content confidentiality and, on a shared contract read whole, interest hiding. It gives nothing against a global observer, and the node that accepts the transaction sees the submitter's IP address. Three weaknesses belong to the ledger itself. First, every ciphertext stays in block bodies and in any archive for as long as the chain is kept. Indexer retention policy is not deletion; MIP-0002 leaves the retention of parsed events to each consumer [@midnight-mip-0002]. The launch profile has no forward secrecy (`DEC-002`), the property that keeps earlier Messages unreadable when a key is exposed later, so a later compromise of a stream secret reads every copy that was ever kept. Next, the fee and contract call of each Message are public, and the publication rate of the lane contract is visible to everyone, block by block. Finally, a public event exposes its emitting contract, entry point, transaction, time and size whatever its payload holds. MIP-0019 says so for encrypted `Misc` payloads: application encryption "does not hide that metadata" [@midnight-mip-0019].

**Option 1 and on-chain private events.** Option 1 as evaluated here does not use the Foundation's planned private events. It carries a Confidential Message as the ciphertext payload of public `Misc` events, which the Phase 1 rules of MPS-0005 (the public events that MIP-0002 and CoIP-0003 implement) permit, because every emitted field is disclosed and the ciphertext is the disclosed value. MPS-0005 Phase 2 plans something different [@midnight-mps-0005]: fields marked `@private` and encrypted for chosen recipients with `encrypt_for`, `proven` blocks that bind them to the transaction by a commitment, and `@topic` annotations for filtering. The Indexer would keep a topics column and a per-wallet relevance table, recording which events concern which wallet, and would offer wallet-specific decryption. Its stated aim is a scanning reduction of over 99 percent. What the filtering party learns about a wallet's interest is not stated, and neither are the key types, the encryption scheme or the size limit. Under Option 1 the Indexer keeps no such record, because every Subscriber reads the whole lane and only the device knows which entries opened. In the terms of Chapter 4, Indexer-side topic filtering and MIP-0002's prefix filters are selective profiles: the client discloses part of its interest to receive less. The requirement set permits them only as labelled opt-ins (`MPE-PUB-016`, `MPE-PRV-013`). Its default is the strict profile, whole-stream reception with local recognition, and Option 1's privacy rests on that profile. Once Phase 2 is specified, this comparison has to be repeated against it.

**Fit with Midnight today.** Option 1 cannot run on mainnet today. Contract events belong to the ledger-9 generation, and mainnet ran ledger 8.0 as of 4 August 2026; MIP-0002 events run on Stagenet, a test network [@midnight-coip-0005; @midnight-mip-0018]. Events arrived with Compact toolchain 0.33, and toolchain 0.35.0 states that it targets ledger 9, which as of 28 September 2026 was not deployed on mainnet.^[LFDT-Minokawa/compact, doc/release-notes/toolchain-0.35.0.md; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] The release support matrix still lists mainnet as node 1.0.300 and on-chain runtime 3.0.0, the generation before contract events.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-docs, docs/relnotes/support-matrix.json, entries `node` and `onchain-runtime` for `mainnet`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] Until ledger 9 is activated on mainnet, Option 1 can be tried on Stagenet but has no production network.

Size limits the lane next. Events whose serialized form exceeds 1 KiB are silently dropped by the virtual machine.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, onchain-vm/src/vm.rs, constant `MAX_LOG_EMITTED`; tag ledger-9.1.0.0-rc.5, accessed 1 October 2026.] CoIP-0003 states a limit of 1 KB at the language level [@midnight-coip-0003]. All `emit` fields must be disclosed, so the payload is a 256-byte ciphertext in the clear. A body larger than one payload needs the multipart convention of MIP-0019, which is Proposed: parts of exactly 256 bytes from one transaction, one physical intent and one execution phase [@midnight-mip-0019]. A physical intent is a sealed bundle of contract calls to which no other party can add. Its guaranteed segment pays the fee and cannot fail, its fallible segments can fail one at a time (Chapter 2), and MIP-0019's execution phase is one such segment. In the specified format a class-0 sealed body fits one `Misc` part, class 1 needs 4 parts, class 2 needs 16, and class 3 would need 64 (derived: 256 / 256 = 1; 1,024 / 256 = 4; 4,096 / 256 = 16; 16,384 / 256 = 64). The lane's part limit (`P-FMT-8`) is 16, so class 3 is refused before any transaction is built. `Misc` events are not indexed by field, so the lane is read by contract address alone, from finalized blocks, at least once. The Indexer's event API is marked beta, and its ledger-8 adapter drops contract logs.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-indexer, indexer-api/graphql/schema-v4.graphql, `contractEvents` marked `@beta`; indexer-common/src/domain/ledger/ledger_state.rs, function `make_ledger_events_v8`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.]

The lane contract was written in Compact and compiled with toolchain 0.35.0, which uses language 0.27.0 and targets a ledger-9 release candidate. Its circuits were sized with the proof system's cost model and its fees derived from the ledger-9 cost model. Nothing was run on a Midnight network, so these figures are compiled and costed, not measured on a chain, and the contract is exploratory code on which the design does not depend. With the ledger-8 toolchain, 0.31.1, the contract fails to compile because `emit` is unbound. Emitting circuit data is expensive to prove, because every non-constant byte of an `emit` payload has to be unpacked from field elements. One part built from a 256-byte argument needs 166,241 rows (k = 18), where k is the base-2 logarithm of the circuit size: a k = 18 circuit has room for 2^18^ = 262,144 rows, and each step in k doubles the size. Four parts in one call need 663,950 rows (k = 20), and sixteen parts scale to about 2.65 million rows, about k = 22. Every proof in the model is at most 4,368 B and every verifying key at most 2,119 B, but the proving keys at k = 18 are 67 to 76 MB, and generating the keys for the one-part circuit took 264 s and 1.8 GB of memory. Proving time was not measured.

Where a lane Message's parts land decides what a failure costs its Publisher. A failed fallible segment discards its events, while the fee, paid by the guaranteed segment, stays spent. A transcript whose modelled cost exceeds the time-to-dismiss budget goes wholly into the fallible segment of its intent. That budget is the work a node may have to do before it can reject a transaction: the larger of 2 µs per transaction byte and 15 ms, less a per-transaction reserve.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, ledger/src/structure.rs, `min_time_to_dismiss` and `time_to_dismiss_per_byte`; ledger/src/construct.rs, `spare_min_time_to_dismiss`; tag ledger-9.1.0.0-rc.5, accessed 1 October 2026.] The 16-part call exceeds it: its transaction of about 12,920 B in the cost model gives about 25.8 ms before the reserve (derived: 2 µs × 12,920 B), against modelled compute of 27.6 ms (derived), so all sixteen parts go into one fallible segment of one intent, which MIP-0019 accepts. A reader therefore counts a lane Message only if its segment succeeded, and a Publisher whose segment fails has paid about 0.188 DUST at genesis prices for nothing and must publish again.

**Performance.** The chain sets a floor under the lane's latency. A transaction waits up to one 6-second block for inclusion and about three more blocks, about 18 s, for finality, and the Indexer serves lane events only from finalized blocks. A lane Message therefore reaches its Subscribers no sooner than about 18 to 24 s after submission (derived: 18 s + 0 to 6 s), before the Indexer's own delay and before proving, whose time for the k = 18 to k = 22 lane circuits is not measured. Chapter 10 records what an exploratory run of this path against a mock ledger suggested, as an unreplicated observation that informed the design and supports nothing here.

Throughput is the binding problem. The lane competes for two per-block budgets, and which of them binds first is not yet settled. The first is block usage, the transaction bytes a block may hold. The node's mainnet parameter file sets it at 1,000,000 B per block,^[midnightntwrk/midnight-node, res/mainnet/ledger-parameters-config.json, `limits.block_limits`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] while the ledger's initial limits set 200,000 B,^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, ledger/src/structure.rs, constant `INITIAL_LIMITS`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] and which value is live is unknown until measured. At 1,000,000 B per 6 s the chain offers about 166,667 B/s. The cost model puts a class-2 lane transaction (16 parts in one call) at about 12,920 B, and each 256-byte part adds about 306 B of block bytes, so a class-0 transaction is about 8,330 B (derived: 12,920 − 15 × 306). The whole chain could then carry about 13 to 20 lane Messages per second if it carried nothing else (derived: 166,667 / 12,920 = 12.9; 166,667 / 8,330 = 20.0). At 200,000 B per block the figures fall to 2.6 to 4.0 per second (derived: 33,333 / 12,920 = 2.6; 33,333 / 8,330 = 4.0).

The second budget is the per-block write budget. MIP-0002 states that events compete with state writes for a shared `bytes_written` budget of 50,000 B per block [@midnight-mip-0002]. With MIP-0002's overhead of about 40 B per event, one part takes about 328 B (288 + 40). Class 1 (4 parts, about 1,312 B) then fits about 38 times per block, or about 6.3 per second for the whole chain, and class 2 (16 parts, about 5,248 B) about 9 times per block, or about 1.5 per second (derived, with no other writers).

Which budget binds depends on how a log is charged. The write budget counts net writes, bytes a transaction adds to state and leaves there; bytes written and then deleted are churn, which costs work but leaves nothing in state. MIP-0002 charges event bytes as net writes, and on its reading the write budget limits the lane. The ledger-9 cost code instead counts a log's bytes as both written and deleted,^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, onchain-vm/src/vm.rs, the `Log` instruction; base-crypto/src/cost_model.rs, `impl From<RunningCost> for SyntheticCost`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] so they cancel out of net writes and count only as churn. The write budget then never sees lane events, and the limit falls back on block usage. A measurement of 1, 4 and 16-part groups on a ledger-9 network will settle the question (Chapter 9).

Of the four whole-chain ceilings, about 13 to 20, 2.6 to 4.0, 6.3 and 1.5 Messages per second, only the first reaches one Shard's target of 10 per second, and only with the lane taking every byte of every block. The requirement set caps the traffic of the Bus Registry, Option 2's contract for memberships and Anchors, at a 10 percent share of block usage (P-ECO-8). Held to the same share, the lane would get 1.3 to 2.0 Messages per second at the larger block-usage figure, and about 0.15 class-2 Messages per second under the write budget (derived: 13 to 20 / 10; 1.5 / 10), at most a fifth of one Shard's target.

**Storage.** Nothing is stored in contract state. The cost moves to the Indexer, which keeps every row for as long as its operator chooses, and to block bodies, which are kept indefinitely by any archive node. The option has no way to make a Message expire.

**Operator burden and incentives.** No new operators are needed, and operationally this is the option's main strength. The burden falls on Publishers as fees, and compute, not block bytes, dominates the fee. At genesis prices one class-0 lane Message (one `Misc` in one call) costs about 0.129 DUST (0.161 with the wallet's margin), and a class-2 Message (16 parts in one call) about 0.188 DUST (0.236). Sixteen separate one-part calls in one intent cost 0.866 DUST, so one call per Message is the cheaper shape for fees and the more expensive one to prove. All of these are derived from the cost model, not measured, and live prices move by up to about 4.6 percent per block.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, base-crypto/src/cost_model.rs, function `price_adjustment_function`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] One NIGHT generates DUST towards a cap of 5 DUST over about a week,^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, ledger/src/dust.rs, constant `INITIAL_DUST_PARAMETERS`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] or about 0.71 DUST per day when the balance is spent as it regenerates (derived: 5 DUST / 7 days). A Publisher sending one Message a minute spends about 186 to 271 DUST per day (derived: 1,440 × 0.129 = 185.8; 1,440 × 0.188 = 270.7) and needs about 260 to 380 NIGHT behind it (derived: 185.8 / 0.714 = 260; 270.7 / 0.714 = 379). Nothing pays the Indexer for serving the stream, because no code path pays indexer operators at all.

**Upgrade path.** The ledger lane does not grow into a low-latency carrier. A 6 s block and about 18 s of finality bound its latency from below, and the 1 KiB event cap bounds its bodies, so every route to lower latency or larger bodies leaves the ledger. The lane keeps two roles. It is a censorship-resistant last resort that needs no cooperation from a Bus Operator, the operator of Option 2's relays, and it is the home of the contract events, public and later private, that a faster carrier may forward.

**Risk.** Low technical risk and high product risk. The mechanism is simple, and its contract compiles. The risks are a ledger-9 activation date that is not known; proving costs of k = 18 to k = 22 per Message whose time is unmeasured; a fee spike from lane traffic that raises the price of every Midnight transaction; a dependency on one Indexer that cannot tell omission from silence; and governance's ability to pause user transactions, which stops the lane with them. MIP-0018 requires consumers to verify indexer-supplied data against authenticated on-chain data before treating it as authoritative [@midnight-mip-0018]. Node-side event visibility (MPS-0007, Proposed, with no MIP) would let a reader do so without trusting an indexer, but the option does not depend on it [@midnight-mps-0007].

## Option 2: Hybrid GossipSub sidecar overlay with ledger anchoring

**Mechanism.** Bus Nodes are separate processes that run beside Midnight nodes, not inside them. They relay MPE Envelopes over GossipSub, a publish and subscribe protocol in which each node keeps full-message links to a small set of peers per topic, its mesh (D = 8 peers in this design), and only announces message identifiers to its other peers. Each Shard has one GossipSub topic. The transport profile requires GossipSub v1.2 behaviour: the v1.1 scoring and mesh rules plus IDONTWANT, a control message by which a node tells its mesh peers that it already holds a message, so that they need not send it. Store Nodes keep Envelopes for their 48-hour lifetime (`DEC-004`) and serve back-fill by Shard and time window only; they keep no chain history. Subscribers download the whole Shard and recognize their Messages locally by a salted 16-byte Recognition Tag inside the seal. The ledger holds a Bus Registry contract with memberships, quota roots and the relay list, and one Anchor per 60-second window. Message bodies never enter contract state. The ledger lane of Option 1 remains as an explicitly chosen, labelled last resort.

One Message shows the path. A Publisher's 300 bytes exceed the 86-byte payload capacity of class 0 and fit the 854 bytes of class 1, so the Envelope is 1,544 B on the wire whatever the bytes say. One Bus Node checks its admission against the quota roots and publishes it on the Shard's GossipSub topic, and mesh peers pass it on. Store Nodes keep a copy for 48 hours, every Subscriber of the Shard tests its Recognition Tag, and only the recipient's device finds a match. When the 60-second window closes, the next Anchor commits to the window's batch, which includes it.

**Privacy delivered.** Sealing gives content confidentiality against relays, Store Nodes, the Indexer and chain observers, under the chosen cryptographic assumptions. Interest hiding comes from whole-Shard reception: every Subscriber of a Shard downloads the same bytes, and the Recognition Tag test happens on the Subscriber's device. Fixed size classes (wire lengths of 776, 1,544, 4,616 and 16,904 B) conceal payload length within a class. The class itself stays visible, and so do message timing, volume and the framing and control traffic of each connection. GossipSub topic names disclose the network prefix, the protocol's major version and Shard membership. With one launch Shard there is no difference among Shards to reveal, but a peer's participation and traffic remain visible.

The overlay itself hides little, because GossipSub's own messages tell neighbours what a node holds and when. GossipSub announces subscriptions to connected peers [@2019-vyzovitis-gossipsub-v01]. Outside its mesh a node periodically sends IHAVE messages listing identifiers it has recently received, and a peer that lacks one answers with IWANT; with IDONTWANT, these control messages tell neighbours which identifiers a node holds and when it first held them. A neighbour can also estimate its distance from a publisher by reaction time, which the protocol "does not try to obscure" [@2020-leastauthority-gossipsub-audit, p. 7]. A colluding minority of relays that compares first sightings can therefore estimate which ingress point an Envelope entered by, and the ingress sees the submitting connection directly. Publishing without a pubsub signature (StrictNoSign, under which a pubsub message carries no author, sequence number, signature or key field) removes the author stamp from the pubsub message, but not the authenticated connection identity, the address or the first-hop observation. A Publisher hands its Envelope to one Bus Node, which publishes it on the Publisher's behalf: a one-hop stem, in Dandelion++'s terms, with no anonymity claim (`DEC-023`). The argument made for Option 1 holds here as well: the overlay adds no source privacy. Interest privacy rests on whole-Shard reception and on client behaviour. Subscriptions, requests, retries, errors and repair must not change with what a Subscriber recognises, and since GossipSub supplies no proof of that property, the client has to keep it.

The properties are Option 1's, held against more adversaries, since relays and Store Nodes join the Indexer and chain observers. One property is added: Store Nodes delete bodies after 48 hours, whereas the ledger keeps them as long as the chain. GossipSub does not read the Envelope's expiry field, and the stock IWANT handlers can serve a cached Envelope without checking it, so an expiry-aware cache is a transport requirement, and the proof of concept must show that its cache enforces it. Expiry bounds what Store Nodes serve; any participant can still keep its own copy.

The admission mechanism planned for the proof of concept is a stand-in whose secrets the relays can see. Until a real anonymous Admission Proof replaces it, the proof of concept offers no unlinkability between a membership and its Envelopes against a relay. The production instrument (`DEC-007`) is a membership with a per-Envelope admission nullifier proof in the style of rate-limiting nullifiers. A member holds a budget of credits per 60-second admission window (64, 16, 4 and 1 Envelopes by class). Each Envelope spends one credit and carries an admission nullifier derived from the member's secret, the window, the class and the credit index. It also carries a share of that secret: one point on a line that the secret and the same inputs fix, taken at a position set by the Envelope. One point reveals nothing about the secret, but two determine it. A credit spent on two Envelopes therefore yields one admission nullifier with two shares, from which anyone can solve for the secret and revoke the member; within the budget, every admission nullifier differs. The instrument must still pass its gates: proof fields of at most 4,096 B and verification within 10 ms. The membership is paid for by the network fee of its registration transaction, because DUST cannot be paid to a contract and a contract cannot observe a price. The Bus Registry's registration circuit compiles, and one registration is about 8.7 KB and 0.246 DUST (0.308 with margin, derived), inside the gate of 16 KiB and 0.5 DUST that the requirement set later adopts (P-ECO-13). Its revocation circuit checks two rate-limiting shares in circuit with `transientHash`, which Midnight does not commit to keeping stable across upgrades, so a production relation needs a hash with that commitment.^[LFDT-Minokawa/compact, doc/api/CompactStandardLibrary/exports.md, `transientHash`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.]

**Fit with Midnight today.** The option needs no change to the node. The node has no GossipSub and offers no plugin point for a new protocol, but nothing in it prevents an independent process from reading the chain through RPC and the Indexer and submitting ordinary transactions.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-node, Cargo.lock, no `libp2p-gossipsub` package; node/src/service.rs, function `new_full`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] The Bus Registry, the ledger-lane contract and a consumer contract were written in Compact and compiled with toolchain 0.35.0; their circuits were sized and their fees derived, and none was run on a network. They are exploratory code, and the design does not depend on their being final. In the Bus Registry, `emit` is the only ledger-9 dependency: without the `Misc` copies of Anchors and its governance events it compiles with the ledger-8 toolchain. Anchors are checked against Bus Registry state, a ring of 2,940 slots holding the live history; the `Misc` copy is a notification, because events are not consensus state. The `Misc` copies, any Anchor history read from logs instead of from the ring, and the ledger lane need ledger 9, as Option 1 does. Compiling the contracts also exposed four limits of today's Compact. A transaction has no signer, so the steward's and the anchorer's authority is a hash preimage proved in circuit. A contract can compare block time but cannot read it, so every time-dependent write takes a caller clock value checked within a tolerance (900 s in the compiled Bus Registry). The 3,600 s window for accepting membership roots cannot be enforced by the contract, so Bus Nodes enforce it. Large transcripts, including registrations and Anchors, land in the fallible segment, which keeps their events in one segment of one intent but outside the guaranteed segment. The overlay part runs on any ledger generation.

GossipSub raises two fit questions of its own: which protocol version two Bus Nodes speak, and how one Midnight network's traffic is kept apart from another's. Together they mean that a Bus Node which both prefixes its protocol identifiers and negotiates v1.2 cannot interoperate with standard GossipSub peers without configuration.

Two libp2p peers opening a stream agree on a protocol identifier, a string that names the protocol and its version. IDONTWANT belongs to v1.2, whose local specification is a Working Draft (revision r2, 30 August 2026), and a link to a peer that speaks only v1.1 runs without IDONTWANT, although the versions are compatible.^[libp2p/specs, pubsub/gossipsub/gossipsub-v1.2.md, lifecycle table; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] The transport profile therefore requires negotiating `/meshsub/1.2.0`. The inspected Rust and Go libraries also offer v1.3 and older versions by default, so a Bus Node must record the negotiated identifier and enforce its version policy.

Separating networks collides with the version rule. Shard topics and request-response identifiers carry a network prefix, `/mpe/<g>/1`, where `g` is the first eight bytes of the genesis hash in lowercase hexadecimal. Stock rust-libp2p accepts only v1.0 or v1.1 as the internal version for a custom protocol identifier,^[libp2p/rust-libp2p, protocols/gossipsub/src/config.rs, enum `Version` and `ConfigBuilder::protocol_id`; protocols/gossipsub/src/protocol.rs, `ProtocolConfig::default`; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] so a prefixed identifier would run without IDONTWANT. The proof of concept will therefore negotiate `/mpe/<g>/1/gossipsub/1.2.0` through a `Version::V1_2` change in a vendored copy of the library.

The prefix does not by itself keep networks apart, and neither does the relay list. Topic names are routing labels, not authenticated identifiers: a peer can use any name, and the default Rust subscription filter limits the number of topics without checking their names. A Bus Node therefore has to accept only supported names for its genesis prefix and the Shards the Bus Registry authorises, and admission has to bind the full genesis hash, so that an Envelope admitted on one network is not accepted on another with the same eight-byte prefix.

**Performance.** GossipSub's designers measured a p99 of 141 ms with 1,000 honest nodes facing 4,000 Sybils (identities run by one attacker) in a network-wide eclipse attempt on a warm network, using the mesh profile this design adopts (D = 8, D_low = 6, D_high = 12, heartbeat 1 s) [@2020-vyzovitis-gossipsub]. Waku's GossipSub relay with rate-limiting nullifiers delivered 2 KB messages in 497 ms on average across 1,000 simulated nodes [@2024-revuelta-waku-latency]. Both are results of particular experiments and guarantee nothing about the protocol, and neither carried Midnight Express's Envelopes, admission or Shard rules. Whether Midnight Express delivers near these figures is the first question the proof of concept has to answer (Chapter 11). Ingress amplification is the bytes a Bus Node receives divided by the bytes of the distinct Envelopes published. IDONTWANT should lower it, because a mesh peer that has been told a node holds a message does not send that message again. Whether it cuts class-3 egress by at least 30 percent, net of its own control bytes, is hypothesis Q5 of Chapter 9, and its saving on wide-area links is unknown.

The requirement set plans bandwidth with a simple model: a Bus Node's egress is 1.25 × D times the Shard's unique byte rate, D copies of every new byte plus a 25 percent margin. The model is a planning assumption and bounds nothing in the protocol. The edge budget of 6 Mbit/s in each direction for one Bus Node (`P-PRF-12`) was derived from it at D = 8: at the byte cap the model gives 10 copies of 65,536 B/s, about 5.2 Mbit/s (derived: 65,536 × 10 × 8 = 5,242,880 bit/s), which leaves about 0.76 Mbit/s of headroom (derived: 6 − 5.24). Whether every Bus Node stays within the budget, and whether amplification falls below 1.25 × D, is hypothesis Q4 of Chapter 9. The proof of concept's local swarms will run real library code but will not reproduce deployment latency, bandwidth, address diversity or failure behaviour, and the latency, throughput and bandwidth values in the requirement set are acceptance targets, not GossipSub properties. Chapter 10 records what exploratory local runs suggested about amplification, as unreplicated observations that informed the design and support none of these figures.

**Storage.** Each Store Node replica of one Shard holds at most 65,536 B/s × 172,800 s, about 11.3 GB, at the byte cap (derived). The requirement set asks for three Bus Operators per Shard. At the baseline mix of Chapter 9 (10 Envelopes per second, 60, 25, 10 and 5 percent of them in classes 0 to 3) the mean wire length is 2,158.4 B (derived: 0.60 × 776 + 0.25 × 1,544 + 0.10 × 4,616 + 0.05 × 16,904), so a Shard carries about 1.86 GB per day (derived: 10 × 2,158.4 × 86,400 = 1,864,857,600 B) and 48 hours is about 3.7 GB per replica. The ledger stores the Bus Registry and the live Anchor history of 49 hours: 2,940 records in a ring of slots indexed by window number modulo 2,940, so the bound holds by construction and anyone can prune an expired record (derived: 2,940 × 60 s = 176,400 s = 49 hours). One Anchor transaction is about 10,340 B (estimated from the compiled circuit's 4,368-byte proof and the transaction's other parts). At 1,440 Anchors per day that is about 14.9 MB of block usage per day, which is 0.10 percent of the 14.4 GB per day the chain offers at 1,000,000 B per block, or 0.52 percent at 200,000 B per block (derived: 10,340 × 1,440 = 14,889,600 B; 1,000,000 × 14,400 = 14.4 GB; 200,000 × 14,400 = 2.88 GB). At genesis prices one Anchor costs about 0.195 DUST (0.244 with margin), or about 280 DUST per day for the anchorer (derived: 1,440 × 0.195 = 280.8), which takes about 390 NIGHT of DUST generation to sustain (derived: 280.8 / 0.714 = 393). Each Anchor also needs a k = 18 proof (152,138 rows), most of it spent on the bytes of its `Misc` payload, so the anchorer produces one such proof every minute. Its proving time is not measured.

The Subscriber pays the largest storage and bandwidth cost: about 1.86 GB per day to receive one whole Shard at the baseline mix. Against the mobile budget of 56 to 60 MB per day that the requirement set later adopts, that is about 31 times the upper end (derived: 1,865 MB / 60 MB = 31.1), so phones cannot receive a whole Shard on that budget. Light reception is an open decision (`DEC-021`), and any selective mode must carry its own, weaker claim.

**Operator burden and incentives.** Here Option 2 is weakest. It needs a new class of Bus Operators to run Bus Nodes and Store Nodes, and Midnight has no way to pay them. DUST is non-transferable, and no code path credits fees to anyone: the block-reward hook in the runtime returns zero.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-node, runtime/src/lib.rs, `impl Get<BlockReward>` returning `(0, None)` in both feature configurations; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] MPS-0004 records the same unsolved problem for proof servers [@midnight-mps-0004]. Any bond held in the Bus Registry would be a contract balance in NIGHT or another token, unrelated to NIGHT staking, which locks nothing and carries no consensus weight [@midnight-mip-0016]. Paying relays for measured quality has proved exploitable: in Nym, performance-score attacks cut the cost of dominating the active set by over 99 percent [@2026-cao-nymreputation, p. 1]. Leaving relays unpaid has its own record. Among 712 Nostr relays surveyed, 95 percent of free relays could not cover their costs [@2024-wei-nostr-empirical]. The decision register therefore funds launch Bus Operators through written off-protocol commitments for 12 months (`DEC-001`) and permissions relays at launch: at least 8 organizations, none with more than 20 percent (`DEC-006`). XMTP took the same route for its broadcast network: a permissioned set of trusted organizations at first, with the chain used only for payloads that need total ordering [@2024-xmtp-xip49-decentralized-backend]. With permissioned relays, collusion among Bus Operators is a governance problem as well as a cryptographic one.

**Upgrade path.** Here Option 2 is strongest, because each later property attaches without replacing the carrier. A mix ingress can sit in front of the stem (`DEC-023`, option (d)), and a private retrieval profile can sit beside whole-Shard reception (`DEC-021`). More Shards add capacity (`DEC-011`). Open relaying can replace the allow-list after a capture red team fails (`DEC-006`). A contract event, public today and private once MPS-0005 Part 2 exists, can travel unchanged as a carried event when the five conditions for carried events hold; here the size condition means 854 B of payload in class 1 or 3,926 B in class 2. Whether Phase 2 meets the first two conditions is unknown until its text is published, and a recognition profile aligned with `encrypt_for` waits for the same text. The design can also be built in stages: Stage 0 is planned to run against a mock Ledger Adapter in the proof of concept, the Bus Registry and its consumer compile, and each production gate is a measurement with its threshold fixed in advance (Chapter 9).

**Risk.** Moderate. Two risks follow from the design itself and have planned responses. A network-wide admission quota cannot be enforced by independent validators. Each Bus Node checks an admission nullifier against those it has seen, so two conflicting Envelopes that spend the same credit and enter at different ingress points can each be accepted before either Bus Node hears of the other, and nothing in mesh connectivity bounds how many such copies get through. The response is detection and revocation instead of prevention (`DEC-014`): the two shares of a spent credit reveal the member's secret, and the revocation circuit compiles. Whether on average at most one Envelope per equivocated admission nullifier reaches Subscribers is hypothesis Q9 of Chapter 9.

Restarts are the second risk. A Bus Node holds the admission nullifiers it has seen in memory, so a restart erases them, and during a 140 s restart barrier it accepts no live admissions, because it cannot yet tell a fresh credit from one spent before the restart. GossipSub's seen cache, the list of message identifiers a node has handled, records an identifier before the application validates the message, and ignoring the message does not remove the entry. The identifier is a hash of the Envelope's wire bytes, so a retry of the same Envelope, from the Publisher or a mesh peer, is dropped as a duplicate while the entry lasts (60 s in the Rust configuration, 120 s by default in Go), and mesh peers do not resend afterwards. A copy that a barrier Bus Node silently ignores must then be recovered by another path, such as a Subscriber's repair from a second Store Node. An explicit Busy reply, which is a Midnight Express service reply and not a GossipSub validation outcome, sends the Publisher at once to another Bus Node instead. Whether delivery under churn reaches 0.999 after the drain, with repair carrying less than 2 percent of pairs, is hypothesis Q8 of Chapter 9, tested with a Busy reply and with silent ignoring; Chapter 10 records what exploratory runs suggested about the two responses, as unreplicated observations.

The risks that no design argument settles are larger: Bus Operator funding beyond the first year, an admission proof that meets its size and time gates, behaviour at hundreds of Bus Nodes on real networks, and an anonymity set that stays small while traffic is thin. Zcash shows what thin traffic does to the last of these: only 0.3 percent of its transactions were fully shielded in the period examined [@2018-kappos-zcash-anonymity, p. 2].

The transport adds open risks of its own, most of them in peer scoring, GossipSub v1.1's defence against misbehaving peers. Each node scores every peer from per-topic terms (time in the mesh, first deliveries, mesh delivery rate and invalid messages) plus penalties outside any topic, and prunes a peer whose score turns negative. A topic cap limits what the topic terms together can add, but it applies after positive and negative contributions are summed, so a peer rewarded on several Shards can hide invalid-delivery penalties on another, and the safety of the chosen score profile is unknown. StrictNoSign requires a Bus Node to reject a pubsub message that carries any author field; rust-libp2p's anonymous validation mode rejects each of them, `key` included, on its master branch,^[libp2p/rust-libp2p, protocols/gossipsub/src/protocol.rs, `ValidationMode::Anonymous` branch of the decoder, error `KeyPresent`; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] and the proof of concept must confirm that the release it pins does the same. D_out is the number of mesh peers a node must have dialled itself; the inspected Go validator requires D_out < D/2 and rejects the chosen D_out = 4 at D = 8,^[libp2p/go-libp2p-pubsub, gossipsub.go, `GossipSubParams.validate`; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] although the specification allows equality, so the profile cannot run on the Go library unmodified, and interoperability between Rust and Go Bus Nodes is not established. Closure of the scoring vulnerability CVE-2022-47547 for the release the proof of concept pins is unknown. None of these favours another option, but each must be cleared before the overlay is opened to the public.

## Option 3: In-node protocol

**Mechanism.** The carrier becomes a protocol inside the Midnight node. The node already carries consensus gossip through the Substrate gossip engine. GRANDPA, which finalizes blocks, and BEEFY, which adds finality statements for light clients and bridges, use that engine with single 32-byte topics and a per-protocol validator that decides what to rebroadcast. A Midnight Express protocol would be registered beside them, or beside the node's own ledger-sync protocol, and would ride the node's existing peer connections. The Bus Registry and Anchors stay on the ledger as in Option 2.

**The case for it.** It reuses what exists: peer discovery, connections, operators and the release process. It needs no new class of operator and no second network to bootstrap, and every Midnight node becomes a potential relay. A reduced form keeps some appeal as a last resort: a notification protocol enabled only on non-validators, behind a flag, shipped only if a sidecar proved impossible to deploy.

**Privacy delivered.** The same as Option 2 if the same Envelopes are carried, because sealing, fixed sizes and whole-Shard reception do not depend on the transport. One property is worse. Validators are a small, known set (10 permissioned seats at mainnet genesis), and Aura, under which committee members author blocks in turn, makes block authors predictable. Carrying Envelopes on those nodes ties relay observations to known operators and known IP addresses.

**Fit with Midnight today.** Poor. The node has no plugin mechanism. Protocols are registered in the service builder, protocol names embed the genesis hash, and the protocol spans the network only if every node operator runs the modified binary. Every Midnight Express release becomes a node release, and every node release passes through Midnight's governance, where a federated motion takes five days.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-node, runtime/src/lib.rs, constant `MOTION_DURATION`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] The Substrate gossip engine is not GossipSub: it has no mesh maintenance, no peer scoring, no IDONTWANT and no flood-publishing control. GossipSub's published protections therefore do not carry over.

**Performance.** Unmeasured, with a specific hazard. Envelope traffic at the Shard byte cap, multiplied by gossip amplification, would share connections and peer slots with block propagation and GRANDPA votes, whose gossip interval is 333 ms. The node configures no peer limits of its own and relies on the upstream defaults of 8 outbound and 32 inbound peers.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-node, node/src/service.rs, `default_peers_set_num_full` passed to the ledger-sync handler; upstream defaults in paritytech/polkadot-sdk, substrate/client/cli/src/params/network_params.rs; accessed 1 October 2026.] A flood of Envelopes becomes a load on consensus.

**Storage.** Node operators would have to keep 48 hours of Envelopes or delegate retention to someone else. A node with the default state pruning keeps trie state only for recent blocks,^[midnightntwrk/midnight-node, node/src/ledger_stats_rpc.rs, trait `LedgerStatsApi`, method `ledger_stats`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] so a 48-hour retention duty would be new to its operator.

**Operator burden and incentives.** Every node operator carries Envelope traffic whether they want to or not, and nobody pays them for it. The incentive problem is Option 2's, now imposed on operators who signed up for something else. MPS-0030 and MPS-0038 describe the validator operating envelope as already constrained [@midnight-mps-0030; @midnight-mps-0038], and for that reason the requirement set that follows from this choice places no duty on block producers (`MPE-OPS-001`).

**Upgrade path.** Every change to the protocol is a coordinated node upgrade, and protocol experiments become chain upgrades.

**Risk.** High, and the main risk is coupling. A bug in the carrier can affect block production, and an attack on the carrier becomes an attack on consensus. A burst of Envelopes that delayed GRANDPA votes would delay finality for every Midnight user, including those who never subscribe to anything. The requirement set that follows from this choice therefore forbids Midnight Express code in the node binary and requires an unmodified node, runtime and pallet set (`MPE-NET-001`, `MPE-NET-003`).

## Option 4: Mix network or PIR-based delivery

**Mechanism.** Delivery goes through the strong-privacy lineage of Chapter 5, while the ledger keeps Option 2's role. Two variants were considered. A mix variant sends Envelopes through a mix network with continuous delays and cover traffic, in the style of Loopix and its deployed descendant Nym [@2017-piotrowska-loopix; @2021-diaz-nym]. Each mix delays, reorders and re-encrypts packets so that an observer cannot match what leaves a mix to what entered it, and cover traffic, made of dummy packets, hides when a user is active. The libp2p mix protocol specifies 4,608-byte Sphinx packets for this purpose [@2024-vac-libp2p-mix-spec]. A retrieval variant has servers hold the Shard and Subscribers fetch their Messages through private information retrieval (PIR) or oblivious message retrieval (OMR). The server then never learns which records a Subscriber wanted, and the Subscriber need not download everything.

**Privacy delivered.** Option 4 is the only option that can claim more than the limited definition. A mix network with enough cover traffic can protect publisher and recipient anonymity against a global passive observer. Single-server information-theoretic PIR requires downloading the whole database [@1998-chor-pir, p. 1], so the retrieval variant gives selective fetching only with computational privacy or with servers that do not collude. The trilemma states the price of both: strong anonymity is impossible whenever 2βℓ < 1, where β is the fraction of users sending noise per step and ℓ is the number of steps a message may stay in the network [@2017-das-trilemma, pp. 1-2]. With one user in ten sending noise in each step (β = 0.1) and a message allowed three steps (ℓ = 3), 2βℓ = 0.6 and strong anonymity is ruled out; ℓ has to reach 5 before the bound stops ruling it out (derived: 2 × 0.1 × 5 = 1). Waku's mix integration recommends a pool of at least 100 mix nodes and states that subscriber anonymity "is not addressed" [@2025-waku-mix-spec]. A mix in front of a publish/subscribe carrier thus protects the publisher, not the reader.

**Fit with Midnight today.** None. Midnight has no mix network and no retrieval servers, and nothing in the protocol pays for either. Nym exists, but it is an external network with its own token and its own reputation problems [@2026-cao-nymreputation], and using it would bring Option 5's dependency problem with it.

**Performance.** Vuvuzela reaches 37 s end to end for a million users, and each client spends about 12 KB/s whether it talks or not [@2015-vandenhooff-vuvuzela, pp. 1-2]. That is about 1 GB per day of cover per idle client (12 KB/s × 86,400 s), already the order of the 1.86 GB per day of whole-Shard reception at the baseline mix. Loopix keeps latency in seconds only with loop and drop cover traffic [@2017-piotrowska-loopix]. On the retrieval side, original oblivious message retrieval costs about 0.065 s of detector time per message per receiver [@2021-liu-omr]. At one Shard of 864,000 Envelopes per day that is about 15.6 hours of detector time per receiver per day (derived: 864,000 × 0.065 s = 56,160 s). Its clue of about 956 B is larger than the smallest Envelope. SimplePIR answers quickly but needs a 121 MB client hint for a 1 GB database [@2022-henzinger-simplepir], and one Shard at the byte cap holds about 11.3 GB over 48 hours.

**Storage.** Retrieval servers keep the whole retention window and process it per query. Mix nodes keep little, but every client sends cover continuously.

**Operator burden and incentives.** The heaviest of the five options. A mix network needs a large, independent and funded node set, because its guarantees rest on enough honest mixes in every path. Retrieval servers spend CPU on every Subscriber's queries, about 15.6 hours of OMR detector time per receiver per day at one Shard, and the multi-server schemes need operators who are trusted not to collude. Midnight has no mechanism to fund either role.

**Upgrade path.** As a primary carrier, Option 4 would have to be built entire before it delivers anything. As profiles on top of Option 2 it is the natural path to stronger claims: a mix ingress protects publishers, and a private retrieval profile serves phones. Each is opt-in and labelled with its own claim.

**Risk.** High for launch: unfunded operators, latency outside the target, and a cover-traffic cost that Midnight users have not accepted. Low as a later profile, because the carrier underneath does not change.

## Option 5: External pub/sub service

**Mechanism.** Midnight uses a publish/subscribe network that already exists. Two forms were considered. One is a relay network of the Waku kind. The Waku Network is itself GossipSub with rate-limiting nullifiers, split into eight shared shards, with membership kept in a contract and a rate limit of one message per second per member [@2024-cornelius-waku-network-dapps]. The other is a federated broker or relay set of the Nostr kind, where clients send signed messages to relays they choose and subscribe with filters.

**The case for it.** It is the fastest route to a working carrier. Its relays and clients already exist, and someone else bears the operating cost. A shared network also has more users to hide among than a new Midnight network would have at launch, which matters given the Zcash evidence on thin anonymity sets.

**Privacy delivered.** It depends on which services the client uses, and the convenient services leak interest. A Nostr client subscribes by sending its relay a filter of authors, kinds and tag values [@nostr-nip-01], and Nostr's direct-message wrapper still tags the recipient's public key for the relay [@nostr-nip-17]. A Waku light client sends the content topics it wants to a filter node [@2020-vac-waku2-filter-spec]. Waku's history service requires the querier to reveal its content filters [@2020-vac-waku2-store-spec]. Each discloses Subscriber interest to infrastructure, the leak this design exists to remove. Used as a dumb pipe with whole-shard reception, a Waku-style relay can deliver the same properties as Option 2. The carrier then depends on clients never using the services that make the network convenient, and the external operators decide what those services log.

**Fit with Midnight today.** Good for code, poor for everything that ties the carrier to Midnight. Admission in Waku is a membership in a contract on Ethereum, and relaying nodes must stay in sync with that chain through an Ethereum RPC provider or their own execution client [@2024-revuelta-waku-latency]. Adopting it ties Midnight's admission to another chain, its fee token and its governance. Midnight contracts could not check that admission, and Anchors would have to be carried back to Midnight by some cross-chain mechanism. Nostr has no admission at all beyond per-relay policy and optional proof of work.

**Performance.** Adequate in simulation. Waku's 1,000-node simulation delivered 2 KB messages with a p95 of 597 ms [@2024-revuelta-waku-latency]. Its rate limit is just below a Midnight Express membership's class-0 budget of 64 Envelopes per 60-second admission window (derived: 64 / 60 = 1.07 per second), and a shared shard is not under Midnight's control.

**Storage.** Set by the external operators' policies, not by the requirement set. Nostr replicates each post across 34.6 relays on average, with 98.2 percent of retrievals redundant [@2024-wei-nostr-empirical].

**Operator burden and incentives.** Low for Midnight, and only apparently so. The external network faces the same funding problem. Free Nostr relays mostly cannot cover their costs [@2024-wei-nostr-empirical], and the Waku Network names incentives for its service nodes as future work [@2024-cornelius-waku-network-dapps]. Midnight would depend on someone else's unsolved sustainability problem and could do nothing about it.

**Upgrade path.** Controlled by the external project. Midnight could not add a size-class format, an admission proof bound to its own ledger, or a contract consumption path without the other network's agreement.

**Risk.** High dependency risk. The privacy, availability and admission of the carrier would all rest on parties outside Midnight's governance, using services that leak interest by default.

Privacy and performance narrow the field; fit, operator burden and risk settle the choice. On privacy, Options 1, 2, 3 and a disciplined Option 5 deliver the same limited properties, and only Option 4 delivers more, at a cost no Midnight participant pays today. On performance, Option 1 is bounded by the chain: a latency floor of about 18 to 24 s before proving, and a whole-chain capacity that reaches one Shard's target only if the lane takes every byte of every block at the larger block-usage figure. The carriers of Options 2 and 5 have published results in the hundreds of milliseconds, from experiments that did not run Midnight Express, and Option 3 has no evidence. Among the remaining criteria, Option 3 couples carrier failures to consensus, Option 5 hands admission and governance to another chain, and Option 4 needs an operator set that nothing funds.

## Recommendation

**Option 2, the GossipSub sidecar overlay with ledger anchoring, is recommended, and it is the architecture of Midnight Express.** It delivers the claimed privacy properties against every adversary class the claim covers, needs no change to the Midnight node, keeps admission on Midnight's ledger, and leaves a path to the stronger properties that only Option 4 offers. Of the options that meet those conditions, it is the only one whose carrier has published delivery results in the hundreds of milliseconds [@2020-vyzovitis-gossipsub; @2024-revuelta-waku-latency], against Option 1's derived floor of about 18 s. Those results are not Midnight Express's own, and whether the system meets its targets is the first thing the proof of concept tests (Chapter 11). It uses the ledger for the records a ledger keeps well: membership, quota roots, the relay list and ordered Anchors. It keeps bodies off the chain, where they would cost fees, proving time, block space and permanence. The recommendation accepts the main point of the case for Option 1. The overlay buys speed, and Store Node retention rules give expiry; neither buys anonymity. It also accepts the cost that case named: a new class of Bus Operators, funded outside the protocol at first.

The recommendation concerns Confidential Messages. Midnight Express complements the Foundation's on-chain events and private events, and under the conditions stated for carried events it transports them. It is not an alternative to them and does not replace MPS-0005 Part 2. Where an application needs a fact to be the contract's own claim, the contract event is the record and a carried copy only delivers it early. Where discovery and recovery must work from chain data alone, as in MIP-0012's custody inbox, the ledger is the carrier and Midnight Express is not offered.

**Option 1 stays as the ledger lane.** The lane is built into the recommended design as a labelled last resort, used only when a Publisher chooses it and never switched on automatically (`DEC-008`). The choice is explicit because a lane Message costs fees, exposes its rate and stays as long as the chain, and an automatic switch would impose those costs on Publishers who never chose them. The lane's latency floor is about 18 to 24 s before Indexer delay and proving time, and it carries a few Messages per second for the whole chain, with bodies only up to class 2. Proving cost may lower that limit further: a 16-part call is a circuit of about k = 22, and the part limit `P-FMT-8` stands at 16. If the lane misses its experimental gate (Q16 in Chapter 9), the limit falls to 4 parts (k = 20) and the lane carries classes 0 and 1 only, unless a cheaper `Misc` encoding appears. The lane needs ledger 9 on the target network; until that generation is activated on mainnet, it can be tried only on Stagenet or against the mock ledger planned for the proof of concept.

Options 3 and 5 are rejected. Option 4 is rejected as a launch carrier and kept as the source of later opt-in profiles: a mix ingress for publishers (`DEC-023`) and a private retrieval profile for light clients (`DEC-021`).

## What would change the choice

The recommendation rests on arithmetic, published results, compiled contracts and stated assumptions, and each of the conditions below would change it.

- **Toward Option 1 as the primary design.** Two findings together would justify this. First, a written product need is met by bodies of at most one 256 B `Misc` payload and by latency of a minute. Second, a measurement on a ledger-9 network shows the lane's traffic within its share of block usage and of the per-block write budget, and the median delay, proving included, within 60 s. That measurement has to report the serialized size, the budget charged and the DUST fee of 1, 4 and 16-part groups, and which budget binds. Failure to secure Bus Operators would also force this outcome: if the procurement settling `DEC-001` cannot find 8 organizations willing to sign for 12 months, Option 2 cannot launch and the ledger lane becomes the only carrier.
- **Toward on-chain private events for a given product.** MPS-0005 Part 2, once published, may give private events an Indexer filter that hides interest at a cost and size a product accepts. For that product the contract event alone would then suffice, and Midnight Express would serve only as an early, interest-hiding carrier of the same bytes. The comparison of Option 1 has to be repeated against the published text, and one Phase 2 private event has to be carried as a carried event under the five conditions above. The pending Confidential Contract Notifications statement may also choose a carrier for confidential data. Its content is unknown, and its choice would bear directly on this recommendation.
- **Against Option 2 on its own terms.** The gate that closes Stage 0 (Chapter 11) is delivery of at least 99 percent with p99 at most 6 s at 200 Bus Nodes. If Midnight Express misses that gate, in the proof of concept or later on real networks, the overlay has not shown the advantage it is chosen for. Midnight Express would also lose its anonymous admission if no Admission Proof meets the gates of at most 4,096 B and 10 ms verification. It would then fall back to issuer-signed stamps, whose issuer learns which customer published which Message (`DEC-007`). A score profile that cannot be shown safe across every supported Shard configuration, or a GossipSub profile on which the Rust and Go libraries cannot agree, would keep the allow-list permanent and narrow the choice of libraries a Bus Node can use.
- **Toward Option 3.** If Midnight ships a supported extension point for new network protocols, isolated from consensus peer slots and released independently of the node, most of the coupling argument goes away.
- **Toward Option 4 profiles.** A product requirement for publisher anonymity against a global observer would make the mix ingress mandatory for that product, and its latency and cover cost would have to be accepted. A requirement that phones receive Messages within the 56 to 60 MB per day budget would make a private retrieval profile mandatory, once a measured OMR or PIR profile shows a cost a Bus Operator will carry.
- **Toward Option 5.** If an existing relay network agrees to carry sealed MPE Envelopes on a dedicated shard, with admission checked against the Bus Registry and no filter or store service exposed to Midnight Express clients, its relays could replace Midnight Express's own. The system would then gain an operated network and a larger crowd, and would keep its own format, admission and Anchors.

Three of these conditions bear on Option 2 directly and can be tested before launch: the Stage 0 gate at 200 Bus Nodes, an Admission Proof within its gates, and written commitments from 8 organizations for 12 months. Chapter 7 states the requirements that hold Option 2 to these conditions, and Chapter 11 orders the experiments that test them, beginning with the proof of concept. Whether all three hold together is open.
