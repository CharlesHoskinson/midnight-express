# The need for private events {#ch3}

Midnight already hides who owns a coin, what a contract's private state holds, and which witness proved a statement. It does not hide anything that a contract emits as an event. Its contract events are public by construction. Private events, which are contract events with encrypted fields, are planned but not yet specified. Midnight also gives applications no confidential way to tell each other that something happened outside a transaction.

## What an event is

On Midnight an event is a contract event, and it is a notification, not a fact. Midnight contracts are written in Compact, and a circuit is one entry point of a contract, compiled so that a zero-knowledge proof can show that it ran correctly without revealing its private inputs. A Compact circuit emits an event with `emit` while the transaction executes. The ledger records it in the transaction transcript (the public record of the operations the call performed, against which its proof is checked), wrapped in a `VersionedLogItem`, and the Indexer serves it to readers [@midnight-mip-0002; @midnight-coip-0003]. The Indexer is the service that "reads chain data and serves it to wallets and DApps" [@midnight-mps-0028].

The Foundation publishes changes as MIPs (Midnight Improvement Proposals) and problems as MPSs (Midnight Problem Statements); Proposed means submitted, and Accepted means approved by its editors. MIP-0002 is the only Accepted proposal about events. It calls events "a notification mechanism, not a state commitment". It also treats each event as the contract author's claim about what happened, not as a fact that a reader can verify from the event itself, and leaves the retention of parsed events to whoever consumes them [@midnight-mip-0002]. A reader who must be certain of a fact therefore checks the contract's state, and uses the event to learn that there is something to check.

MPS-0005 plans events in two phases: Phase 1, the public events that MIP-0002 specifies, and Phase 2, private events [@midnight-mps-0005]. Of the parts it lists for Phase 2, a `@private` field annotation, `encrypt_for` for encrypted delivery and `proven` blocks for commitment binding define the event itself. The others help a recipient find its events, and so decide what the Indexer learns about each recipient: a `@topic` annotation for filtering, and Indexer support through a topics column, "wallet-specific decryption with session isolation" and a `RelevantContractEvent` table. A private event is therefore still a contract event: it is produced by contract execution inside a proved transaction, some of its fields are encrypted for chosen recipients, and a commitment that the proof checks binds them to the transaction. A commitment is a short value computed from data that fixes the data without revealing it; because the proof checks it, nobody can later present different fields as the ones the transaction emitted. MPS-0005 is Proposed, and it says that Phase 2 is scoped in a Part 2 whose text is not published, so the syntax, key types, encryption scheme and leakage of private events are unknown. Midnight Express does not redefine them.

Midnight Express carries a third kind of object. A Message is an application message that a Publisher seals and delivers: a short, self-contained statement that something has happened or is wanted. An order was filled, a loan fell below its collateral threshold, a credential was revoked, a payment is waiting, an agent has an offer. Some of these report what a contract did. Many, such as offers, invoices and alerts, were never emitted by any contract. Three properties separate a Message from the other things a blockchain system carries.

A Message is not a transaction. A transaction changes ledger state and must be ordered, validated and kept, whereas a Message reports a change or an intention and is useful only for a limited time: an alert that a loan is under its threshold is of no use to the borrower once the loan has been liquidated. If a reaction is needed, the reaction is a separate transaction. The requirement set gives Messages a lifetime of 48 hours (172,800 s) by default, with an optional paid archive of seven days. A contract event, by contrast, lives in the transaction that emitted it.

Nor is a Message a conversation. A chat message is written for one reader or a small group that knows itself, and expects a reply. A Message is one-to-many and decoupled, in the publish and subscribe pattern: the Publisher need not know who listens, and a listener need not have contacted the Publisher before.

Messages are also small, with a bounded size. Most Messages that applications on Midnight need fit in a few hundred bytes. The requirement set allows four payload capacities, 86, 854, 3,926 and 16,214 B, and anything larger travels by reference. Take the loan alert above, carrying a 32-byte loan identifier, a 16-byte amount and an 8-byte deadline. That is 56 B, and it fits the smallest capacity of 86 B. Add a 32-byte reference to the lending contract and it becomes 88 B, two bytes over, so it moves to the 854-byte capacity.

The loan alert has two things to hide: the loan it names, and who is waiting for it. Midnight Express seals each Message inside an MPE Envelope of one of four fixed sizes (MPE stands for Midnight Private Events, the prefix of the requirement set's identifiers), and only parties holding the stream's secret can open it. A stream is one flow of related Messages, such as one market's offers or one wallet's invoices, and its secret is a key shared by the parties allowed to read it. A short pseudorandom Recognition Tag lets the intended reader recognise the Message without trial-decrypting everything, that is, without attempting to decrypt every Envelope to see which ones open.

MPE Envelopes travel on a Shard, a partition of the Message flow carried as one GossipSub topic, and every reader of a Shard receives all of it. GossipSub is the publish/subscribe protocol of the libp2p networking library: each peer subscribed to a topic keeps a small set of neighbours for it, about eight in this design, and passes every message it receives on to them. Here the peers are Bus Nodes, processes that run beside Midnight nodes and relay MPE Envelopes. Follow one Envelope to one Subscriber, the reader's process that receives the Shard. The Subscriber reads the Envelope's salt, computes from its stream secret and that salt the 16-byte Recognition Tag it would expect, and compares it with the Recognition Tag in the Envelope. If the two differ, the Envelope belongs to another stream and is dropped unopened. If they match, the Subscriber decrypts the body and checks the Publisher's signature inside it.

A Confidential Message is a Message that holds four properties, which Chapter 4 defines. The first is content confidentiality. The second is sealed labels: no stream name, recipient address or session identifier appears in clear. The third is conditional interest privacy: a Subscriber that receives its complete Shards and recognises locally behaves identically on the network whatever its interests. The Subscriber above keeps it only if dropping one Envelope and opening another look the same on the network. The fourth is no silent change: no other retrieval path, overload path, ledger lane or application reaction changes this leakage unless the application opts into it. The ledger lane (Option 1 of Chapter 6) posts sealed MPE Envelopes on the ledger as encrypted contract events, which show every chain reader their emitting contract and time; a Message goes there only by the application's choice.

A Confidential Message is not a contract event, and no contract executed it. The Publisher's signature, inside the seal, authenticates it, and an Anchor commits to it. The Bus Registry is the Compact contract on Midnight that lists the registered Publishers with their quotas and the Bus Nodes allowed to relay and to post Anchors. Each MPE Envelope carries an Admission Proof that its Publisher is registered there and within its quota for the current 60-second admission window, and Bus Nodes relay only Envelopes whose proof checks. An Anchor is a record in the Bus Registry by which an authorised anchorer commits to the identifiers of the MPE Envelopes it accepted in one 60-second window, on every Shard. The Bus Registry checks the anchorer's authority, the window's timing bounds and the record's form, not the committed identifiers, so the chain establishes only that the commitment existed at the finalized block that includes it.

A contract that later reacts to a Message must check this commitment without learning which Message it was. Hash trees (Merkle trees) allow that: a short path of hashes from a leaf to the root shows that the leaf is present without listing the others. The identifiers of the MPE Envelopes that one Shard accepted in a window hash to that Shard's batch root, the batch roots of all Shards hash to the 32-byte window root that the Anchor records, and the Bus Registry keeps each live Anchor as a leaf of an Anchor tree of depth 12. Anchors stay live for 49 hours (the 48-hour lifetime plus one hour of margin, Chapter 7): at one per minute that is at most 49 × 60 = 2,940 Anchors, within the tree's 2^12 = 4,096 leaves. A contract compares only the Anchor-tree root with the Bus Registry, and the path, which would identify the Envelope, stays a private input of its proof.

A carried event is a Confidential Message whose payload is the unchanged bytes of a contract event, public or private, together with the transaction hash and position that let a Consumer check it against the chain. Its authority stays with the transaction that emitted it. Midnight Express is a complement to private events and, under conditions that Chapter 4 states, a transport for them. It is not an alternative to them.

## Who publishes and who consumes

A Subscriber receives the MPE Envelopes of a Shard and recognises its Messages; a Consumer is the party that acts on a Message. For an agent or a wallet the two are the same process. For a contract they are not, because a contract cannot receive anything by itself. The Foundation uses "consumer" more widely, for any party that processes events, including indexers, wallets and explorers [@midnight-mip-0002; @midnight-mip-0018]. In Midnight Express a Consumer may also be a contract, which consumes only through a later transaction. Four kinds of party differ in what they publish, how they consume and what limits them.

| Party | Publishes | Consumes | Binding constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autonomous agent or back-end service | Offers, requests, status, alerts, as Messages | Continuously, reacts in seconds | Long-running, can afford a full Shard download; holds its own keys and wallet |
| Smart contract | Contract events, through the transaction that calls it (public today; private events planned) | A Message only when someone submits a later transaction that carries it as a private input | Cannot read Messages or events, cannot call out, cannot hold a secret key, can compare the block time but not read it |
| Wallet | Payment notices, invoices, replies | Intermittently, often on a phone | Holds the user's keys; limited bandwidth and uptime; today exposes no event or Message interface to applications |
| Application (DApp front end or server) | On behalf of its users | On behalf of its users | Must not see keys for streams it has not been granted |

Agents are the most demanding Consumers and the easiest to serve. An agent runs for days, watches several streams, and must react before an opportunity passes. It can download a whole Shard. At the per-Shard limit of 64 KiB per second, a full day of one Shard is at most 64 × 86,400 = 5,529,600 KiB, about 5.7 GB (derived); a server can afford that, and a phone cannot. An agent may hold a wallet of its own and act for a human owner within delegated limits. MPS-0015, a Proposed problem statement, records that AI agents are arriving on Midnight and that Midnight City already runs agents in simulation [@midnight-mps-0015]. An agent's Message traffic is a direct view of its owner's financial behaviour.

Contracts are the most constrained. A Compact contract runs only inside a transaction. It cannot subscribe to anything, poll anything or keep a private key in its public state. A Midnight transaction has no signer, so any authority a contract checks must be proved in circuit, for example as knowledge of a hash preimage, a secret whose hash the contract stores. A contract can react to a Message only when some party (the Message's recipient, an agent, or anyone paid a bounty) submits a later transaction that presents the Message as a private witness. The requirement set records this as `MPE-CON-042`. Every contract use case therefore needs an off-chain carrier and a proof that does not reveal which Message triggered the reaction. A contract can act on a signed instruction carried in a Message; proving to a contract that the Message was admitted and anchored by a deadline needs a binding between the signed statement and the anchored Envelope that version 1 does not specify.

An exploratory circuit that checks inclusion under an Anchor has been compiled; the signature check that would give a reaction authority has not been written. The consumer contract, a Compact contract that reacts to Messages, has a reaction circuit that checks a Message's inclusion under an Anchor-tree root and inserts a consumption nullifier, which marks the Message as used: the contract records it when it acts on a Message and refuses any later reaction that produces the same value. The consumption nullifier is a hash over, among other inputs, the consumer contract's address and a secret that the submitter supplies as a private input. Readers of the stream derive that secret from the stream secret, one for each Message; a hash of the public Envelope identifier alone would let anyone match each reaction to its Message. The contract was written in Compact 0.35.0 (language 0.27.0) for a ledger-9 release candidate, compiled and costed, but not run on any network. It is exploratory code, and the design does not depend on it being final.

A circuit compiles to a table of constraints that the proof system checks, and each line of the table is a row. The reaction circuit needs 77,905 rows (k = 17), with a proof of 4,368 B and a verifying key of 2,119 B. The figure k is the base-2 logarithm of the circuit's table size: k = 17 means 2^17 = 131,072 rows, the smallest power of two that holds 77,905. Proving time and memory grow with k, while the proof and the verifying key stay small. A reaction carries two contract calls, because the consumer contract asks the Bus Registry whether the Anchor-tree root is valid.

The circuit discloses the Anchor-tree root, a coarse expiry bound chosen by the prover, the consumption nullifier and the action. It does not disclose the Envelope identifier, the window, the Shard or the paths. The coarse bound is needed because every comparison with the block time puts its operand in the public transcript, and a precise expiry would fix the second at which the Message was created: anyone who saw an expiry of 09:00:12 could subtract the 48-hour lifetime. The prover therefore discloses a coarser bound, any time after the current block time and no later than the expiry: the expiry rounded down to the hour reveals only that hour, and a bound just past the current block time reveals less (Chapter 4).

Inclusion alone is not authority. The compiled circuit proves that some anchored Message exists and that the consumption nullifier is new, but it does not check that the submitted secret is the one derived for that Message, so the submitter can choose it. A Subscriber holding an inclusion proof could therefore react several times, each time with a different secret and so a fresh consumption nullifier. A Publisher signature checked in circuit, over a statement that names the contract and the action, would close this gap if the circuit also checks that the submitted secret belongs to the signed Message and recomputes the payload digest from the bytes the effect uses: the reaction would then carry a signed instruction. It would still not prove that the overlay admitted or anchored that Message, because version 1 does not bind the signed statement to the anchored MPE Envelope. Chapter 8 describes the contracts; the signature circuit has not yet been written or compiled.

Wallets sit between agents and contracts. A wallet holds the keys and is the right place to recognise Messages addressed to its user, yet it is also the component with the least bandwidth and the least uptime. The Midnight DApp connector, the interface through which an application talks to a wallet, offers balances, addresses, transaction balancing, signing, submission and configuration, but no event or subscription method.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-dapp-connector-api, src/api.ts, type WalletConnectedAPI; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] An application that wants events today must open its own connection to the Indexer that the wallet reports, and so must hold whatever selector it uses for its query, a contract address or a key, which the Indexer then sees.

Applications publish for their users and display what arrives. The requirement set allows a wallet to grant an application access to named streams (`MPE-CON-052`, an open production requirement), so an application never holds keys for streams it was not given.

## What public events leak

A public contract event leaks in three layers: its content, its context, and the pattern of who reacts to it.

Compact accepts only the standard event structures. The one custom structure, `Misc`, has a 32-byte name and a 256-byte payload, and all fields must be disclosed before they are emitted.^[LFDT-Minokawa/compact, compiler/midnight-events.ss, standard event definitions; and coips/coip-0003.md, section "Why disclosure enforcement?"; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] Every event also carries the contract that emitted it, the entry point and the transaction it belongs to. It is authenticated to that contract and entry point, not to the user who triggered the call [@midnight-mps-0043]. The Indexer's contract-event subscription serves it from finalized blocks (finality is the point after which a block can no longer be reverted), about three 6-second blocks, about 18 s, after inclusion, plus indexing delay [@midnight-mps-0028]. The transcript that holds the event is public from the moment its block is seen, so observers of included but unfinalized blocks can read it earlier [@midnight-mip-0002]. Submission, inclusion and finality are distinct moments.

The first layer is content. The payload says what happened. In an auction it shows the bid. In a loan it shows the collateral ratio, and in a payment notice the amount and the invoice. Encryption removes this layer, and only this layer. That holds for an application-encrypted `Misc` payload today, and it will hold for the encrypted fields of a private event.

Context, the second layer, survives encryption. Even a ciphertext in a `Misc` event tells an observer which contract emitted it, from which entry point [@midnight-mps-0043], at what time and at what size. MIP-0019 states this for encrypted payloads: "Application encryption may hide payload plaintext but does not hide that metadata or retract disclosed data" [@midnight-mip-0019]. Size alone is enough to classify traffic. Coull and Dyer infer user actions, message language and message length with more than 96 percent accuracy from the sizes of encrypted packets alone in Apple iMessage, and show that the same attack applies to WhatsApp, Viber and Telegram [@2014-coull-imessage-privacy]. Padding that hid lengths cost them over 300 percent overhead. That overhead is why Midnight Express uses four fixed size classes rather than padding each Message to its own length. Four size classes make the length identical inside a class. The class itself stays visible, and an observer who knows how applications use the classes can still tell those uses apart. The emitting contract is a stronger label still: an event from a lending contract is a lending event, whatever its ciphertext says.

The third layer, reaction and interest, is the deepest. It comes from what happens around the event: who fetched it, who reacted, how soon, and with what follow-up transaction. Consider one encrypted event from a lending contract, followed a minute later by a transaction that adds collateral to one position. Nobody opened the ciphertext, yet an observer can infer that a position was at risk and which owner acted on it. Measurements on deployed privacy systems show that this layer undoes much of the hiding that the cryptography provides.

Zcash's shielded pool is cryptographically sound, yet Kappos and co-authors link 65.6 percent of the value withdrawn from the pool back to deposits by founders or miners, using deposit and withdrawal patterns. With one further heuristic they reduce the overall anonymity set (the group of users among whom an observer cannot tell who made a given transaction) by 69.1 percent [@2018-kappos-zcash-anonymity]. Stealth addresses leak through the same layer. A stealth address is a one-time address that the payer derives for each payment, so that the recipient's public address never appears on chain. On Ethereum, Kovács and Seres identify the real recipient of 48.5 percent of Umbra stealth-address payments on mainnet, and of 25.8, 65.7 and 52.6 percent on Polygon, Arbitrum and Optimism. Most of the identifications come from the pattern in which a recipient collects several stealth payments into one address [@2023-kovacs-umbra-anonymity]. The ERC-5564 standard that generalises stealth addresses publishes each announcement as a public contract event whose `caller` field is indexed, and warns that the wallet funding a stealth address must have no link to its owner [@2022-erc5564-stealth].

Subscriptions leak in the same way. A Bloom filter is a compact bit array that matches every item in a set and, with a tunable probability, some items outside it. Bitcoin light clients hand a Bloom filter of their addresses to a full node, so that it forwards only relevant transactions, and rely on the false matches to hide the real addresses. Clients that use fewer than about 20 addresses leak almost all of them, and the leak worsens when the client restarts or the adversary collects more than one filter [@2014-gervais-bloomfilters]. Later measurements of the two deployed light-client designs show the same trade between privacy and communication [@2024-kotzer-bitcoinlightclients]. A subscription filter sent to infrastructure is a statement of interest.

In Signal, sealed sender hides the sender of a message from the server. Martiny and co-authors show that a statistical disclosure attack, which correlates over many rounds who receives messages while a target is active, links sealed-sender users in as few as five messages when helped by delivery receipts [@2021-martiny-sealed-sender]. A large service also learns the population: Gegenhuber and co-authors probe more than a hundred million phone numbers per hour against WhatsApp's contact discovery without effective rate limiting, and take a census of the service [@2026-gegenhuber-whatsapp-enumeration].

Two results bound what any design can claim, including this one. Biryukov, Khovratovich and Pustogarov link Bitcoin transactions to the IP addresses that originated them using only a few machines connected to the peer-to-peer network [@2014-biryukov-deanonymisation]. Heimbach and co-authors locate more than 15 percent of Ethereum validators in the network over three days by watching which peers forward which attestations in GossipSub [@2024-heimbach-deanon]. Midnight Express runs GossipSub between its Bus Nodes, and GossipSub gives no anonymity of its own. The specification makes no claim to anonymity or protection against traffic analysis [@2020-gossipsub-v11-spec]: a peer forwards a message as soon as it has checked it, with no added delay, mixing or cover traffic. Bus Nodes inherit this. The Bus Node that first receives an MPE Envelope sees the connection that submitted it, and neighbouring Bus Nodes see forwarding paths and the times at which they received each Envelope. For this reason the privacy claims in Chapter 4 cover content confidentiality, and interest privacy against infrastructure within the set of Shards a client receives. They make no claim against an observer who watches the first hop or correlates timing at the network layer.

## Where confidential Messages are needed

None of the four applications below needs anonymity against a global observer. All of them need the content kept from infrastructure, and interest hidden from it.

### Agents trading in a shared world

Midnight City runs AI agents in a simulation, a live public testbed [@midnight-mps-0015].

Suppose, as an illustration that goes beyond what Midnight City implements today, that an agent in such a world, with a private wallet, wants to buy a quantity of a resource. It publishes a request for quotes, several agents answer with offers, and it accepts one and settles. If the request, the offers and the acceptance are public contract events, every other agent learns the buyer's demand before it is met and can trade ahead of it. Every agent whose offer was refused learns the accepted price. Any observer can draw up a graph of who trades with whom, and over many rounds a profile of each owner's holdings and habits. Shielding the settlement payment does not help, because the events before it have already said what it will be. Private events would hide the fields of each request and offer, but they would still name the market contract, the entry point and the time of every step, and each step would wait for a transaction and finality.

With Midnight Express the request goes out as a Confidential Message on a shared Shard. Only agents holding the market's stream secret can read it, and offers return as sealed replies on the same Shard. If the request and the offers fall in the same size class (a request of 300 B and offers of 500 B both fit the 854-byte capacity), infrastructure cannot tell a request from an offer by its length, or one market from another on the same Shard. Timing and volume remain visible: a burst of Envelopes just after another can still mark a round of trading. Each agent downloads the whole Shard and recognises locally, so no Bus Node learns which market it follows, provided that nothing the agent does on the network depends on what it recognised. The Bus Node that receives each offer still sees the connection that submitted it. A counterparty who reads the request still learns its content: Midnight Express hides the Message from infrastructure, not from its intended readers.

### A payment that needs a message

Midnight's shielded transfers hide the coin's owner and value. The output ciphertext in a shielded transaction is fixed and small, though: one curve point and six field elements, enough to describe the coin and nothing more.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, zswap/src/structure.rs, type CoinCiphertext; tag ledger-9.1.0.0-rc.5, accessed 1 October 2026.] Real payments come with other information: an invoice before the payment, a reference or memo with it, a receipt or refund address after it. Today that information has no confidential carrier on Midnight.

The Foundation's own proposals record the same gap. In the shielded token standard, contract-initiated outputs carry no coin ciphertext, so "the returned coin info is the recipient's only copy; callers SHOULD deliver it out of band" [@midnight-mip-0011]. The problem statement on shielded source of funds asks whether provenance evidence should be "encrypted to the recipient, provided out-of-band, selectively disclosed, or issued as a verifiable attestation" [@midnight-mps-0025]. An offer file (MIP-0005) is a proven, deliberately unbalanced transaction, shared off chain for a counterparty to complete; no part of it can hold a note from the maker, so offer files "carry no messages" until a later upgrade adds them [@midnight-mip-0005]. The atomic-swap standard already publishes offers outside Midnight, on a data-availability layer (a separate network that stores data for anyone to read) under one namespace that every application reads [@midnight-mip-0006]. Each of these needs a channel for data that does not belong in a transaction. The custody standard is the counter-case. It rejects out-of-band delivery because discovery and total-loss recovery must work from chain data alone, and keeps an on-ledger inbox instead, an append-only map of encrypted coin descriptions in the custody contract's public state [@midnight-mip-0012]. Where that property is required, Midnight Express is not a substitute.

A merchant who posts an invoice as a public contract event links the invoice amount to the time of the shielded payment that follows. A chain observer can then match the two, in the way the Zcash heuristics matched deposits and withdrawals. If the invoice goes through the merchant's own server, the server learns every customer relationship, as the WhatsApp census shows a large service will. With Midnight Express the invoice is sealed to the payer and recognised by the payer's wallet from its Recognition Tag, and honest Store Nodes (Bus Nodes that also keep MPE Envelopes for Subscribers who were offline) delete it after 48 hours. An invoice of about 200 B fits the 854-byte capacity and travels as a 1,544-byte MPE Envelope. Expiry does not delete copies that an adversary recorded. An observer sees one MPE Envelope and, later, one shielded transaction, and can relate them only by timing, which the design does not claim to hide.

### A contract that must tell a holder something

Several Midnight contract patterns produce a fact that one party needs to learn: a lending contract recording that a position has fallen below its threshold, an issuer revoking a credential, a sealed-bid auction closing, an escrow releasing funds. A public contract event tells the whole chain that this contract has just produced this kind of fact, and the time and the follow-up transaction usually narrow the affected party down. A margin call broadcast in public invites others to trade against the position before the borrower acts.

This is the case private events are planned for. A private event would let the contract encrypt the fact for the holder and bind it to the transaction, so that the holder has the contract's own authenticated statement. It would still show which contract emitted something, from which entry point and when. It would reach the holder only after finality, through an Indexer whose topic filtering and wallet-specific decryption have unknown leakage [@midnight-mps-0005]. Midnight Express adds to this in two ways. When the contract's own authority is needed, a carried event can bring the private event's exact bytes to the holder early and without revealing the holder's interest to Bus Nodes and Store Nodes; the read that later confirms the bytes still tells the Indexer which contract the holder follows. It can be early because the party that assembles the transaction holds those bytes and can publish them on submission rather than after finality. An early copy has no authority of its own, so the holder treats it as unconfirmed until the transaction is final and the bytes match the transcript. That check rests on the three of Chapter 4's conditions that bear directly on private events: the bytes must exist when the transaction is assembled, or there is nothing to publish early; the transcript must hold the encrypted fields, or a commitment to them, for the holder to compare the carried bytes with; and the event, with its transaction hash, position, contract address and entry point, must fit a size class. Whether Phase 2 meets them is unknown until its specification is published. When the fact does not need a contract's authority, the service that runs the contract's application, or an agent watching it, publishes a Confidential Message to the holder, and an Anchor later commits to the window that contains it.

If the holder must then make the contract react (top up collateral, claim a refund), the holder submits the consumer contract's reaction transaction. The chain sees a reaction, the coarse expiry bound and the Anchor-tree root, but not which Message caused it, and as compiled the proof does not show that the holder is the authorised Consumer; the planned check of the Publisher's signature would add that authority.

Posting the Anchors has a cost. One Anchor per 60-second window covers all Shards, so a day needs 1,440 of them (24 × 60). The exploratory Bus Registry's Anchor circuit compiles to 152,138 rows (k = 18), so the anchorer, the party that assembles and posts each Anchor, produces one k = 18 proof each minute. The derived fee is about 0.195 DUST per Anchor (0.244 with the wallet's fee margin) at genesis prices, or about 280 DUST per day (1,440 × 0.195). The live Anchor records stay at 2,940; the Anchor tree's set of accepted roots still grows by about 1,440 roots (about 46 KB) a day until a reset rule, still open, bounds it. The 0.195 DUST per Anchor assumes no net state growth; each retained Anchor-tree root adds about 32 B, about 0.006 DUST per Anchor or about 9 DUST per day (derived: 32 × 0.0002; 1,440 × 0.0064), until a reset rule bounds the tree. DUST cannot be transferred, so its cost is the NIGHT held to generate it: about 393 NIGHT (derived: 1,440 × 0.195 = 280.8 DUST per day; 280.8 / 0.714 DUST per NIGHT per day = 393), assuming generation runs at the full rate while the balance stays below the cap. Chapter 8 describes the anchoring path; these figures come from compiling the contract and from the ledger cost model, not from a network.

### A group that needs an alert feed

Some streams have many readers and no single recipient: a price or risk feed paid for by a closed set of subscribers, an alert channel for a group of validators or Bus Operators (the registered parties that run Bus Nodes, Store Nodes and the anchorer), a coordination channel for a committee voting off-chain before it submits a result. The content may be harmless; who reads it is not. A public feed shows every reader's interest to the Indexer that serves the contract address. A broker service shows it to the broker. A feed sealed under a group stream secret on a shared Shard shows neither, provided that each member downloads the whole Shard and recognises the feed locally. It still shows that each member uses Midnight Express and which Shards each member receives. Chapter 4 states the limits on removing a member, who keeps the stream secret after leaving until the group rekeys.

## Why existing Midnight mechanisms do not meet the need

Midnight offers four mechanisms today that might carry events or Messages, and plans a fifth.

Contract events come first. The `emit` mechanism is the obvious carrier, and it is public by intent. The Compact proposal that introduced it limits `emit` to values that are already disclosed. It names private emission as "a different problem with different tradeoffs (encryption, topic-based filtering, recipient key management)" and leaves it out of the first version.^[LFDT-Minokawa/compact, coips/coip-0003.md, section "Why disclosure enforcement?"; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] MIP-0002 agrees: events of Phase 1 carry values that are already public [@midnight-mip-0002]. The capacity is the 256-byte payload of `Misc`. MIP-0019, a Proposed standard, joins several 256-byte parts from one transaction and one intent (a group of contract calls within a transaction) into one package [@midnight-mip-0019]. MIP-0002 caps a single event at 1 KB of serialized bytes. The virtual machine in ledger 9 counts differently: the ledger stores a value as a tree of storage nodes, and the virtual machine adds a 32-byte hash per node to the data the nodes hold, then silently drops any event whose total exceeds 1 KiB.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, onchain-vm/src/vm.rs, constant MAX_LOG_EMITTED; tag ledger-9.1.0.0-rc.5, accessed 1 October 2026.] An event spread over many nodes can serialize under 1 KB and still exceed 1 KiB once its hashes are counted, so a Publisher must size each part against both caps.

Contract events are also not yet on mainnet. The published documentation describes the earlier ledger generation, in which contract events do not exist. On 4 August 2026 mainnet (the production network) ran ledger 8.0, while preview (a public test network) had moved to ledger 9.1 [@midnight-coip-0005], and MIP-0002 is live on Stagenet, a staging network [@midnight-mip-0018]. The Compact 0.35.0 release notes state that ledger 9 was still not deployed on mainnet as of 28 September and direct developers targeting mainnet to toolchain 0.31.x, which rejects `emit`.^[LFDT-Minokawa/compact, doc/release-notes/toolchain-0.35.0.md, section "High-level summary"; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.]

An event is emitted inside a proved transaction; one call can emit several events and one transaction can hold several calls, so transaction and proof overhead can be shared, while each logged byte pays `Log` gas and the transaction pays its fee in DUST. Events also draw on the chain's shared block budgets. MIP-0002 sizes event capacity against the 50,000 B per-block `bytes_written` budget at an event's serialized size, about 40 B above its struct data, so a `Misc` part counts about 328 B (288 B of struct data plus 40) [@midnight-mip-0002]. The ledger-9 code charges a logged event as both written and deleted, and the block limit applies to net written bytes, so under the code an event adds nothing to `bytes_written`, its bytes count as churn, and `block_usage`, the budget for the total size of a block's transactions, binds. Which reading governs a live ledger-9 network is unmeasured (`MPE-PRF-040`). `block_usage` is 1,000,000 B in the Midnight node's network configuration files and 200,000 B in the ledger's initial limits, and which value is live is also unmeasured. Under MIP-0002's sizing a four-part class-1 Message writes about 1,312 B, so 38 fit in a block, about 6.3 per second for the whole chain with no other writers (3.5 if `block_usage` is 200,000 B); a sixteen-part class-2 Message writes about 5,248 B, so 9 fit, about 1.5 per second. Planning uses the lowest figure for each class: about 4.0 class-0, 3.5 class-1 and 1.5 class-2 Messages per second for the whole chain. All these rates are derived at one block per 6 s.

Proving is costly too. In the exploratory ledger-lane contract, compiled with the same toolchain, emitting one 256-byte `Misc` from a circuit argument needs 166,241 rows (k = 18), four parts in one call need 663,950 rows (k = 20), and sixteen parts need 2,651,779 rows (about k = 22). Turning circuit values into bytes dominates that cost. From the ledger's cost model at genesis prices, one `Misc` alone costs about 0.129 DUST (0.161 with the wallet margin), sixteen parts in one call about 0.188 DUST, and sixteen separate calls about 0.866 DUST. These fees assume a 4,368 B proof for every contract call, an upper bound for the ledger lane: the compiler's model gives the lane circuits 2,800 B proofs, which bring the sixteen-call shape to about 55,170 B and 0.62 DUST, while the one-call shapes keep their compute-bound fees.

Midnight divides a transaction's effects into a guaranteed segment, which has a small fixed budget and carries the fee, and a fallible segment, which can fail without undoing the guaranteed one. The transcript of a sixteen-part call exceeds the guaranteed segment's budget, so the whole transcript, events included, lands in the fallible segment. The parts of one Message therefore share one segment of one intent, but not the guaranteed one, so a Publisher whose fallible segment fails has paid for a large Message that is never emitted. These figures were derived from compilation and the cost model, not run on a network.

Encrypting the payload before emitting it is the basis of the ledger lane. It removes the content leak and leaves the context leak intact: emitting contract, entry point, time and size. A ledger-lane Message reaches its reader through the Indexer, which serves finalized blocks, so it arrives no sooner than three 6-second blocks, about 18 s, after inclusion, and up to 6 s later when the transaction waits for the next block (derived from the block time and finality depth). A delay of that size rules the ledger lane out for agents trading in seconds and is acceptable for a labelled lane that an application opts into.

MPS-0005 Phase 2 would add encrypted fields, commitment binding and topic filtering to contract events [@midnight-mps-0005]. It gives something Midnight Express cannot: a field that the contract itself emitted and that the transaction's proof binds. Each emission would still travel inside a proved transaction, whose overhead several events can share, pay `Log` gas (the ledger's fee for emitting an event) and a planned encryption fee, draw on the chain's shared block budgets and wait for inclusion and finality, and the contract, entry point, time and size stay visible. MPS-0005 states the receiver's problem: "Fully encrypted events without metadata force wallets to download and trial-decrypt every event on chain." Its answer is topic filtering at the Indexer with "99%+ scanning reduction", wallet-specific decryption and a per-wallet relevance table. A filter applied by a server works only if the server can tell which items match a reader, and a server that can tell that knows what the reader follows. What the Indexer learns under that model is unknown, because the Part 2 text is not published. The requirement set's default excludes any relevance mapping held by infrastructure by design (`MPE-PUB-009`, `MPE-PUB-010`). A filtered mode of that kind would be a selective profile (reception of part of the traffic, with weaker interest privacy), which an application must opt into (`MPE-PUB-016`, `MPE-PRV-013`). Private events also carry only what contracts emit: an agent's offer or a merchant's invoice is not a contract event.

Indexer subscriptions come next. The Midnight node discards ledger events today. MPS-0007, a Proposed problem statement with no MIP, asks for them to be exposed [@midnight-mps-0007]. Contract events are served to readers by the Indexer, which reads finalized blocks. The Indexer's `contractEvents` query and subscription require a contract address. Its `fieldPrefixes` filters work on standard events only, and `Misc` is not indexed unless an Indexer is configured to do so by policy [@midnight-mip-0002].^[midnightntwrk/midnight-indexer, indexer-api/graphql/schema-v4.graphql, input ContractEventFilter; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] A reader tells the Indexer's operator which contract it follows each time it subscribes. The `unshieldedTransactions` subscription likewise requires an address. The older server-assisted wallet path, `connect(viewingKey)`, hands the viewing key (which lets its holder see a wallet's incoming shielded coins without spending them) to the Indexer, which then trial-decrypts every transaction for that wallet. All three reveal interest to infrastructure. The requirement set forbids this: no stream, Recognition Tag, recipient, key or recognised-Envelope selector is ever sent to infrastructure (`MPE-PUB-010`). Bloom-filter light clients fail the same way [@2014-gervais-bloomfilters].

The current shielded wallet does better by scanning. It subscribes to the global `zswapLedgerEvents` stream, which carries the outputs of Zswap, Midnight's shielded-coin protocol, from its last position and trial-decrypts every output locally, so the Indexer learns nothing about which coins are the wallet's.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-wallet, packages/shielded-wallet/src/v1/Sync.ts, zswapLedgerEvents subscription; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] Fetching everything and recognising locally is the right principle, and the requirement set adopts it as whole-Shard reception by default (`MPE-PUB-011`). But the stream carries coin ciphertexts, not application Messages, and nothing can be added to it without a ledger change. Scanning everything also has a cost that grows with traffic. Monero added a one-byte view tag so that a wallet can skip the full check on about 255 of every 256 outputs, cutting scan time by an estimated 50 to 70 percent [@2021-monero-mrl73-viewtags]. ERC-5564 adopted the same idea and reports about a sixfold reduction in parsing time [@2022-erc5564-stealth]. The salted Recognition Tags of this design give Messages the same property: for each Envelope a Subscriber computes one 16-byte Recognition Tag per stream secret it holds and decrypts only on a match, and the cost per key is one of the quantities the proof of concept will measure. A Recognition Tag serves the purpose of MPS-0005's topic filtering, but each Recognition Tag is used for one Envelope only, Recognition Tags cannot be linked to each other, and only the Subscriber evaluates them.

Midnight nodes use libp2p, but consensus gossip runs through the Substrate gossip engine, and the node has no GossipSub or other publish/subscribe protocol. Adding one means forking the node and asking every operator to run the fork, with Message traffic sharing peer slots and bandwidth with consensus. Chapter 6 weighs that option (Option 3) and does not recommend it. Midnight Express instead runs Bus Nodes beside the Midnight node, not inside it.

Each mechanism meets a different subset of the needs that the four applications set.

| Mechanism | Hides content | Hides interest from infrastructure | Latency | Carries application Messages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Public `emit` | No | No (reader names the contract) | Finality, about 18 s or more | Yes, 256 B per `Misc` event |
| Encrypted payload in `emit` (ledger lane) | Yes | No (reader names the contract) | Finality, about 18 s or more after inclusion | Yes, small; about 3.5 class-1 Messages per second for the whole chain at the lowest reading (6.3 under MIP-0002's `bytes_written` sizing) |
| Planned private event (MPS-0005 Phase 2) | Encrypted fields only | Unknown (topic-filter leakage unspecified) | Finality, about 18 s or more | Contract events only; size unknown |
| Indexer subscription by address or viewing key | Not applicable | No | Finality | Only what is on chain |
| Global Zswap stream with local decryption | Yes | Yes | Finality | No, coin data only |

No mechanism hides content, hides interest from infrastructure, carries application Messages and delivers before finality. The last row shows that local recognition works on Midnight. What is missing is a carrier that does the same for application Messages before finality and keeps the other leaks at a stated minimum.

The Foundation has already placed this question. MPS-0043 says it is the access-control slice of a pending problem statement, Confidential Contract Notifications (PR #271). It assigns to that statement "concealing that a disclosure happened, letting a reader find what was granted without being told, and choosing how the data travels" [@midnight-mps-0043]. That text is not public, so its content is unknown. Chapter 4 closes with this document's own problem statement in the Foundation's format, numbered MPS-xxxx until a Foundation editor assigns a number. Whether MPS-xxxx is Confidential Contract Notifications, a companion to it or a replacement for it is for the Foundation's editors to settle.

## What is lost without a confidential carrier

Without a confidential carrier, application developers have four choices, and each costs something.

They can publish events in public. Then a Midnight application keeps its state private and gives away its behaviour: who trades, when, at what price, and who reacts. The Zcash and Umbra measurements show how much privacy that undoes, and the events around Midnight's shielded transactions would expose them in the same way.

They can wait for private events. That gives a contract-emitted fact with hidden fields, bound to its transaction. It leaves each event's context visible, keeps every notification at transaction cost and finality latency, leaves what the Indexer learns unknown, and does nothing for Messages that no contract emits.

They can run their own servers. Each application then runs a private relay that sees every user's relationships and online times, cannot be checked by the user, and is a single point of failure and censorship. End-to-end encryption does not stop a central service from learning who uses it, who talks to whom and, from sizes and timing, much of what is said [@2026-gegenhuber-whatsapp-enumeration; @2014-coull-imessage-privacy; @2021-martiny-sealed-sender]. Each application also gets its own small population of users, so an observer who sees an MPE Envelope on its relay learns which application the user is using; a shared carrier pools every application's traffic into one anonymity set, and separate servers split it.

They can stay off-chain entirely. Then contracts cannot act on signed instructions carried in Messages, wallets have nothing standard to recognise, and every agent framework writes its own transport.

Midnight Express does not remove every leak. It does not hide that a party uses it or which Shards the party receives. The Bus Node that first receives an MPE Envelope sees who submitted it, and timing and volume remain visible to anyone who watches the network. Its interest privacy holds only while everything a client does on the network (requests, retries, repair, receipts, errors) does not depend on what it recognises. Expiry removes Envelopes from honest Store Nodes, not from adversaries who recorded them. The proof of concept will use a stand-in Admission Proof whose secrets the Bus Nodes can see, so every Bus Node will be able to link every MPE Envelope to the membership that published it; a production proof system must replace it. The authority of a Confidential Message is a Publisher's signature and an Anchor, not contract execution, so Midnight Express complements private events and can carry them, but cannot stand in for them. Two questions remain: the proving cost of the circuit that checks the Publisher's signature, and whether MPS-0005 Part 2 meets the conditions for a carried event.
