## Decentralized publish/subscribe overlays and content-based routing

These systems route by topic or by content, so their routing state records who wants what. Midnight Express keeps the delivery machinery, removes that signal and pays for it in bandwidth. None of these works settles how far one Shard can grow before it must be split.

@2011-carzaniga-contentbased :: A position paper on content-based networking. It argues that publish/subscribe notification (transient, pushed by the producer) and on-demand content retrieval (persistent, requested by the consumer) need different network primitives. The test case is a notification service built from named-data interests (requests for content by name). A consumer cannot know when the next notification will appear, so it keeps re-issuing interests. Each one leaves a pending-interest entry in every router on its path, and most come back empty, so traffic and router state grow with the polling rate and not with the number of notifications delivered. Midnight Express draws the same line for the same reason: live gossip, pushed through Bus Nodes, carries new Envelopes, and back-fill, requested from Store Nodes, serves what a Subscriber missed. The paper's routers, however, match predicates against visible content, which is the interest leakage that sealed Envelopes remove. The paper is short, has no measurements and does not address privacy.

@2012-setty-poldercast :: PolderCast builds a topic-based publish/subscribe overlay from one gossip-maintained ring per topic, plus random shortcuts from two peer-sampling services (gossip protocols that hand each node a changing sample of others): Cyclon for uniform links and Vicinity for interest-based links. It was simulated with Twitter and Facebook subscription traces and Skype churn traces (nodes joining and leaving), at up to 10,000 nodes, 10,000 topics and 5,000 topics per node. Against Scribe, a tree-based overlay, it delivered completely without churn, with logarithmic hop counts. Its "relay-free" goal, in which only a topic's subscribers carry that topic, is the opposite of what Midnight Express needs, because a node's links then reveal its interests. The evaluation assumes honest nodes.

@2016-onica-confidentialitypreserving :: A survey of confidentiality techniques for broker-based publish/subscribe, in which a broker sees every publication and subscription. It classifies trust and access-control models, encrypted matching (asymmetric scalar-product-preserving encryption, homomorphic schemes) and the leakage each one accepts. Its central observation is that if brokers cannot evaluate encrypted subscriptions, routing becomes impossible and all filtering moves to the subscribers. Midnight Express takes that branch on purpose. Bus Nodes forward whole Shards and Subscribers recognise their own Envelopes locally; the cost is bandwidth (about 1.86 GB per day per Shard at the baseline mix of Chapter 9, derived), and the gain is interest-hiding. The survey covers brokers, not gossip overlays, and dates from 2016, when homomorphic matching was impractical.

@2021-antunes-pulsarcast :: Pulsarcast is a topic-based publish/subscribe module for libp2p and IPFS. It stores topics and messages in a Kademlia DHT as a content-addressed Merkle DAG and sends messages down per-topic dissemination trees, with persistence and eventual delivery as its aims. On 100 IPFS nodes replaying Reddit comments, subscription coverage reached 99 percent in normal conditions. With 500 ms latency and 300 ms jitter injected, it fell to at most 86 percent, against at most 41 percent for plain flooding (floodsub). So a libp2p overlay can keep a durable log beside live delivery, which is the role of Store Nodes here; an Anchor adds only an authorized anchorer's commitment to the Envelope Identifiers of one 60 s window. The results are preliminary, come from a five-VM testbed and ignore adversaries.

## Blockchain-based publish/subscribe and ledger message carriers

A ledger gives ordering, durability and a public record. It also makes every byte public and every write paid. Midnight Express puts membership, quota roots and batch Anchors on the ledger and keeps Message bodies out of contract state. How much the ledger lane, its slower second carrier, can carry on the live chain remains open.

@2018-ramachandran-trinity :: Trinity attaches MQTT publish/subscribe brokers to a Tendermint chain. A message published at any broker is validated by a smart contract, ordered by consensus, stored on the ledger and delivered from every broker. On a 20-node Raspberry Pi 3 testbed the validated path added about 3 s: its delay reached about 3.5 s at 5 transactions per second, against about 90 ms for direct loopback delivery. That is what it costs to send every event through consensus, and it is the cost profile of Chapter 6's Option 1 (ledger and Indexer only). Nothing is confidential, since every validator reads every message. The testbed is small, runs on one LAN and uses default Tendermint settings.

@2019-lv-privacy-pubsub-blockchain :: A brokerless publish/subscribe scheme on Ethereum. Publishers post a key and a topic value to the chain, and subscribers send a subscription transaction to the publisher. Topic matching uses public-key encryption with equality test, which compares ciphertexts without decrypting them, so only the publisher learns the subscribed topic. Payloads are ElGamal-encrypted. Message traffic lives in transactions, as it does not in Midnight Express, and anonymity rests on address pseudonymity. Yet each subscription is a visible transaction from subscriber to publisher, and it exposes the relationship. The evaluation measures per-message encryption time only, not throughput, fees or chain load.

@2020-savolainen-streamr-network :: The Streamr Network is a publish/subscribe overlay in production. Each stream is its own random regular graph with four connections per node, built by BitTorrent-like trackers, and tracker addresses and stream permissions are registered in Ethereum smart contracts. On up to 2,048 nodes across AWS regions, propagation delay grows logarithmically, and Dijkstra's algorithm over the tracker's map predicts it with 3.5 percent mean absolute error. Citing eclipse attacks (surrounding a victim with attacker neighbours) and hub attacks on peer sampling, the paper gives topology control to trackers, where GossipSub lets peers choose their neighbours. In exchange, a tracker knows every participant in its stream. The authors include the operator's staff.

@2024-aztec-private-events-note-discovery :: Aztec's documentation for private events and note discovery on a zero-knowledge rollup. Events are encrypted to named recipients and delivered on-chain (in Ethereum blobs) or off-chain by the application. Each log carries a tag, poseidon2(secret, index), siloed per contract: a hash of a secret shared by one sender and one recipient and of a count of that sender's logs. The wallet scans up to 20 indexes beyond the highest finalized one. A recipient thus asks a node for exactly its own logs, and the documentation states what that exposes: the node sees the client's IP address and which tags it asks for. Midnight Express accepts the opposite cost. Its Recognition Tags are salted per Envelope, so a Subscriber takes the whole Shard and tests each tag locally, and no node learns which Envelopes it wants. This is product documentation, not reviewed work.

@2025-liu-phantom-events :: A study of forged smart-contract events on EVM chains, which the authors call "phantom events" and present as the first in-depth security analysis of them. The term names a gap: the chain records which contract emitted an event, not whether the event describes a real state change. The study models five attack types, among them events from a look-alike contract and from functions without access control, and a detector combining bytecode, source code and transaction data finds real cases of all five. One forged-transfer attack on 9 February 2024 cost 1.04 million USD. A Consumer must therefore check a carried event against its recording transaction (the emitting contract address and entry point, the transaction hash, the position and the finalized transcript), never trust its name or shape (`MPE-CON-058`). Only public EVM logs are covered.

@2026-esposito-verbeth :: Verbeth carries end-to-end encrypted messages in Ethereum event logs. All ratchet state stays in the clients, and topics rotate with that state so that conversations cannot be linked. First contact uses trial decryption of handshakes or a private signalling service in trusted hardware. Measured costs are 29,850 gas for a 145-byte message and 82,150 gas for 1,450 bytes, about 40 gas per extra byte (52,300 gas over 1,305 bytes), with plaintext padded to power-of-two buckets. Of current work, it makes the strongest case for carrying messages in the ledger, which buys durability and non-repudiation. That case assumes the chain has room for every message. On Midnight the room is exactly what is in doubt. Contract events arrive there only with ledger 9, the version Chapter 2 targets, and they are public and silently dropped above 1 KiB. Midnight Express therefore uses the ledger only as a slower second lane, the ledger lane, whose capacity is known only as a range: about 1.5 to 12.8 class-2 Messages (4,096-byte sealed body) per second for the whole chain with no other writers (derived, Chapter 8).^[MIP-0002 sizes event capacity against the 50,000 B per-block `bytes_written` budget at about 328 B per `Misc` part [@midnight-mip-0002], which allows nine class-2 Messages per block, about 1.5 per second. The ledger-9 code charges a logged event as both written and deleted, so under the code an event adds nothing to `bytes_written` and `block_usage` binds: 15 class-2 transactions per block (about 2.5 per second) at 200,000 B, or 77 (about 12.8 per second) at 1,000,000 B. Which reading governs a live ledger-9 network, and which `block_usage` value is live, is unmeasured (`MPE-PRF-040`).] A design that puts every message on the chain, as Verbeth does, would have to fit inside that range. Verbeth is a preprint, and its gas figures date from its writing.

## Deployed messengers and their audits

Deployed systems fail mostly outside the core cryptography: in who controls group membership, what a delivery receipt reveals, where load and cost gather, and whether padding is exact at its boundaries. Two of the attacks on Signal and WhatsApp need nothing but timing.

@2019-jacob-matrix-glimpse :: A 2019 crawl of the public Matrix federation. It found 2,003 homeservers and 131,463 users, with 87 percent of users on the top 1 percent of servers. Each server sends a separate transaction to every other server in a room, and sending concentrates: the largest server sent 88.4 percent of all server-to-server transactions and received 0.6 percent, so it took part in 44.5 percent of all traffic in both directions. The authors conclude that this will not scale as the federation spreads out, and they propose per-room gossip or multicast. The crawl is evidence that federations recentralise and that full-mesh fan-out breaks down, which counts against Option 5 (an external pub/sub service). It is a partial snapshot.

@2020-jefferys-session :: The Session whitepaper describes a messenger that needs no phone number. Its storage and onion-routing layer is a permissionless network of staked nodes, grouped into swarms of 5 to 10 nodes that hold the offline messages of the accounts mapped to them. The threat model admits that a malicious node can read headers, log timestamps and refuse to serve messages for chosen account identifiers. Session runs the token-staked relay and storage operators that Option 5 depends on. Its swarms see which account they are serving; Store Nodes hold a whole Shard and do not. It is a vendor document, revised in 2024, with no measured privacy evaluation.

@2021-martiny-sealed-sender :: Shows that Signal's sealed sender, which hides the sender of each message from the server, fails over a conversation. A statistical disclosure attack counts, over many rounds, who sent just before whom received. With delivery receipts (on by default and impossible to switch off), it links sender and recipient in as few as five messages in the authors' models, even for users behind Tor or a VPN. The authors give provably secure fixes and estimate their cost at under 40 USD per month for millions of users. Midnight Express makes the choice this attack exploits. At launch it leaves timing exposed and claims no relationship privacy, and any acknowledgement or reaction that follows soon after a Message gives the same signal. The evidence comes from analysis and simulation.

@2022-trailofbits-simplex-audit :: Trail of Bits' public security assessment of the SimpleX Chat messenger and its cryptographic library, against one 2022 commit. It reports four findings. Two are medium: X3DH (Signal's initial key agreement) secrets used without HKDF key derivation, and key material kept in unpinned memory that is never cleared. Two are low: the pad function's 16-bit length prefix is wrong for messages over 65,535 bytes, and the unpad function throws an exception on very short input. Fixed size classes, which Midnight Express uses, give their privacy only if padding and unpadding are exact at every class boundary, so those code paths need boundary tests. This is a code audit, not a design review.

@2023-albrecht-matrix :: Practical attacks on Matrix and its Element client, in which a malicious homeserver breaks the confidentiality and authentication of end-to-end encrypted rooms. The simplest attacks need no cryptanalysis at all. Because the homeserver controls the list of users and devices in a room, it can add a device it controls, and clients then share their Megolm group session keys with that device. Other attacks exploit missing domain separation in out-of-band verification. In Midnight Express, membership and quota roots therefore come from the ledger, never from the infrastructure carrying traffic. The attacks target the specification and the 2022 JavaScript SDK.

@2024-gegenhuber-careless-whisper :: Shows that delivery receipts in WhatsApp and Signal (automatic replies when a message arrives), which users cannot switch off, let anyone who knows a phone number ping a target silently and often. On WhatsApp the authors sent one reaction every 50 ms and hit no rate limit. Round-trip times reveal whether the app is in the foreground (about 350 ms on WhatsApp) or idle, how many companion devices are active and which operating system they run; the same pings drain battery and data. A Subscriber or contract that reacts predictably to a recognised Message gives the Publisher the same oracle. The results concern mobile clients.

## Mixnets, DC-nets and PIR-based messaging

These systems hide who sends, who receives and when. Midnight Express does not. They pay in one of three ways: every client sends in every round, messages wait in mixes behind cover traffic, or servers compute over the whole database for each read. It is open which of these prices a Publisher sending on a schedule, such as a contract or an agent, could pay.

@2012-wolinsky-dissentnumbers :: Dissent scales DC-nets (group broadcast in which shared secrets cancel out, leaving only the message) with an anytrust split between many clients and a small set of servers, at least one of which must be honest. Communication cost is then linear in the number of clients, not quadratic. On DeterLab it supports more than 5,000 online participants, with latencies as low as 600 ms for groups of 600 clients, two orders of magnitude beyond the 40 to 50 members of earlier DC-net systems. The anonymity needs every client to send in every round, which Midnight Express does not ask of unsynchronised Publishers such as contracts and agents. Dissent is a 2012 prototype that relies on server assistance.

@2015-vandenhooff-vuvuzela :: Vuvuzela hides who talks to whom by passing messages through a chain of servers. Each server adds cover traffic (dummy messages that hide when real ones are sent), sized with differential privacy (a formal bound on what one user's actions reveal), and privacy holds as long as one server is honest. On 36-core EC2 servers it supported 1 million users at 37 s end-to-end latency and 68,000 messages per second. Clients used 12 KB/s (about 30 GB a month: 12 KB/s × 30 days) and servers 166 MB/s. The bound holds even over years of use. The cover traffic stays affordable because an observer of the conversation protocol can count only two things per round, how many users are in a conversation and how many are not, and never learns which users. The authors report that such noise was infeasible for earlier systems, which expose many variables. Messages are limited to 240 bytes. Midnight Express exposes many more variables (each Envelope's size class, entry time and entry peer) and at launch accepts neither the delay nor the constant client traffic.

@2015-corrigangibbs-riposte :: Riposte is an anonymous broadcast board run by a few servers; three are enough. Each client writes a fixed-length message into a shared table by sending shares of a distributed point function, which works as private information retrieval (PIR: reading one record without the server learning which) run in reverse. A multiparty check rejects malformed writes and so does the Admission Proof's job: rejecting abuse without identifying the writer. A three-server cluster builds an anonymity set (the users among whom a sender cannot be told apart) of 2,895,216 users in 32 hours. The design suits workloads with many readers, few writers and high tolerance for delay, and contract and agent Messages do not fit that profile. It assumes the servers do not collude.

@2016-angel-pung-1 :: Pung hides content and metadata from fully untrusted servers, even against a global adversary. Clients deposit messages under labels agreed with their contacts and fetch them with computational PIR, made cheaper by multi-retrieval, the power of two choices and batch codes. Four servers handled 135,000 messages per minute for 32,000 active users. That is 1,000 times more users than earlier systems with the same threat model, but 85 times fewer than Vuvuzela, whose weaker model needs only one honest server. Pung's clients pay 3.8 to 11 MB of traffic per message for a single retrieval and 4.5 to 36 MB with multi-retrieval. Midnight Express claims less still: nothing against a global observer, and its Bus Nodes see when and from where an Envelope enters the network.

@2017-piotrowska-loopix :: Loopix is a mix network of Poisson mixes, which delay each message independently at random, arranged in layers. Clients and mixes send cover and loop traffic, and providers hold messages for offline users. It claims sender and receiver unobservability against a global passive adversary (one that watches every link but alters nothing) and detects active attacks with its loops. A relay handles more than 300 messages per second with under 1.5 ms of processing on top of the chosen mixing delays, and end-to-end latency is a few seconds. Without cover traffic and delays, gossip relaying exposes Publisher timing to a well-placed observer; Midnight Express accepts that exposure at launch. Loopix underlies the mix-network branch of Option 4 (mix network or PIR-based delivery) and the mixnets deployed today. Its performance tests ran on EC2, not on a deployed user population.

@2017-piotrowska-annotify :: AnNotify is a private notification service. In each epoch a publisher derives a notification identifier with a PRF (a keyed function whose output looks random without the key), keyed by a secret shared with the subscriber, and stores it in one of several shards, each a Bloom filter on an untrusted server. Subscribers fetch their shard and a dummy shard through an anonymity network. The adversary's advantage is bounded in terms of the number of shards, the honest queriers and the epochs observed. With 10 Kb shards the yearly cost is about 3 cents per client at both 100,000 and 1 million users, against 60 and 120 cents per user respectively for the bandwidth of DP5, a PIR-based notification service. Midnight Express launches with one Shard, so no Subscriber reveals a Shard choice. Once there are many, AnNotify shows how to bound what that choice reveals. It assumes Tor and accepts Bloom-filter false positives.

@2020-cheng-talek :: In Talek, a private group messaging system, users write to and read from shared logs held by a few servers under an anytrust model. Information-theoretic PIR and blocked cuckoo hashing (which limits where each message may sit) keep the servers from learning which log is accessed. A three-server cluster reached 9,433 messages per second for 32,000 active users at 1.7 s end-to-end latency. Its private notifications tell a user which logs hold new messages without polling them, which is the problem of recognising one's own Envelopes on a Shard. Talek costs periodic client reads and requires that not all servers collude.

@2021-diaz-nym :: Nym's whitepaper describes a permissionless, token-incentivised mixnet in the Loopix family. Mix nodes earn rewards through "proof of mixing", a validator chain runs membership and rewards, and NYM tokens convert into anonymous credentials that prove a right to use a service without linking uses. The paper names a gap that Midnight Express also leaves open: peer-to-peer broadcast exposes IP addresses and timing even when the ledger uses zero-knowledge proofs. Option 4 or a later sender-hiding path could use Nym as a deployed mixnet, though, as Chapter 6 notes, it would bring an external token and Option 5's dependency with it. Its credentials would fill the role of the Admission Proof. It is a company whitepaper, co-authored by IOHK's chief scientist, and it states design goals and reports no measurements.

@2023-gaballah-intersectionmicroblogging :: Shows that anonymous broadcast systems leak, over time, who is publishing. Each time a pseudonym posts, a global observer keeps only the users then online, so churn shrinks the set with every post. The usual fix makes every user send all the time. The authors instead group users with similar publishing habits into anonymity sets and give each set a schedule of cover messages; on Twitter and Reddit traces, set size then shrinks slowly. Midnight Express offers no timing privacy at launch, so its periodic Publishers, agents and contracts, are the easiest targets for intersection. The results are simulated and assume users keep to the schedule.

@2023-langowski-trellis :: Trellis is a mix-net anonymous broadcast system with cryptographic guarantees against full network surveillance. Users send messages in rounds along verifiable random paths built from anonymous routing tokens. Servers post the mixed messages to a public board, and blame protocols remove servers that misbehave. With 64 servers in four regions it reaches 220 bits per second with 100,000 users, and 320 bits per second with 128 servers. That is three to five orders of magnitude faster than Atom (an earlier anonymous broadcast system) but 100 to 1,000 times slower than Tor, and it is today's cost of hiding Publishers in a broadcast; the authors place its use at "enterprise" scale. It assumes that only a fraction of servers is malicious.
