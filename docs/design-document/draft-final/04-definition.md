# Defining private events and Confidential Messages {#ch4}

A design can only be assessed against a definition, and "private" means different things to the people who will use, run and review Midnight Express. A wallet developer expects that nobody but the intended recipient reads the payload. A privacy researcher asks who can tell that two parties are communicating. A Bus Operator wants to know what it can see. The phrase "private event" already belongs to the Midnight Foundation, which uses it for a planned kind of contract event. Midnight Express delivers something else, the Confidential Message, and its definition has to say what it protects, against whom, and where the published literature limits any design at this cost.

## Terms

Two words, *event* and *private event*, belong to the Foundation, and Midnight Express does not redefine them. The Foundation publishes three kinds of document. Midnight Improvement Proposals (MIPs) specify, Midnight Problem Statements (MPSs) pose open problems, and Compact Improvement Proposals (CoIPs) propose changes to the Compact language. A Proposed document is still under discussion. Of the Foundation's event documents, only MIP-0002 is Accepted; MPS-0005, MPS-0007, MPS-0043 and MIP-0019 are Proposed.

An *event* is a contract event: a structured, typed record that a Compact circuit emits with `emit` during execution. The ledger records it in the transaction transcript as a `VersionedLogItem` inside `EventDetails::ContractLog`, and the Indexer serves it through its `contractEvents` query [@midnight-mip-0002; @midnight-coip-0003]. `Misc` is MIP-0002's catch-all event type: a 32-byte name and a 256-byte payload [@midnight-mip-0002]. The transcript is the public record of what one contract call did. Because the transaction's proof is checked against it, the recorded event is exactly what the submitted transaction carried [@midnight-mip-0002]. MIP-0002 calls events "a notification mechanism, not a state commitment", and states that they "represent the contract author's claim about what happened, not independently verifiable facts" [@midnight-mip-0002]. A public event is authenticated to the contract and entry point that emitted it, not to the user who caused the call [@midnight-mps-0043].

MPS-0005 plans contract events in two phases: public events first (Phase 1, which MIP-0002 specifies) and events with encrypted fields second (Phase 2). Phase 1 events carry only disclosed values. In Compact, a value that depends on a user's private input becomes public only where the circuit marks it with `disclose`. Where MPS-0005 differs from MIP-0002 and CoIP-0003 on Phase 1 events, the latter two are normative.

Three kinds of data need separate definitions, because each takes its authority from a different place. Their short forms are these.

**Private event.** A contract event some of whose fields are encrypted for chosen recipients and bound to the transaction by a commitment (a short value that binds the ciphertext without revealing it) that the proof checks, as planned for Phase 2 of MPS-0005 [@midnight-mps-0005]. A private event is defined by Midnight, not by this design.

**Confidential Message.** An application message that a Publisher seals into a fixed-size MPE Envelope, the sealed wire object of Midnight Express, and delivers through Midnight Express, such that the four properties defined below hold: content confidentiality, sealed labels, conditional interest privacy and no silent change. A Confidential Message is not a contract event. It is authenticated by its Publisher's signature and committed by an Anchor, not by contract execution.

An Anchor is a ledger record, written every 60 seconds, that commits to the MPE Envelopes accepted in that window. It dates a Message to a window and says nothing about what a contract computed.

**Carried event.** A Confidential Message whose payload is the unchanged bytes of a contract event, public or private, together with the transaction hash and position that let a Consumer verify it against the chain.

Suppose a contract settles a trade and the buyer must learn the fill price. If the contract emitted the price in an encrypted field, the price would be a private event as MPS-0005 plans it: the chain would hold a commitment to the field, and the proof would show that the field is the one the contract computed. If instead the seller's client sends the price through Midnight Express, the price is a Confidential Message; it carries the seller's signature, and nothing on chain says that the contract computed it. Finally, if a client copies the bytes of an emitted event into an MPE Envelope with the transaction hash and the event's position, the result is a carried event, which arrives early and which the buyer can later check, byte by byte, against the transaction.

The supporting terms keep these meanings throughout the document, the requirement set and the proof of concept.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Message | The application message a Publisher wants delivered: a payload plus the sealed metadata that authenticates it (stream, sequence number, creation time, schema). A Message is not a contract event. |
| MPE Envelope | The fixed-size sealed wire object that carries one Message. It has an 8-byte visible header, a 512-byte admission slot and a sealed body of one of four size classes, numbered 0 to 3 (256 B, 1,024 B, 4,096 B or 16,384 B), giving wire lengths of 776, 1,544, 4,616 and 16,904 B and payload capacities of 86, 854, 3,926 and 16,214 B. The ledger's `VersionedLogItem` is a different object. |
| Shard | One GossipSub topic partition of the overlay. One Shard operates at launch. |
| Recognition Tag | A 16-byte keyed pseudorandom value, salted per MPE Envelope, that lets a Subscriber holding the recognition key recognise its Messages without decrypting every MPE Envelope. |
| Publisher | A client that seals a Message into an MPE Envelope and submits it to a Bus Node. On the ledger lane it emits the sealed body as `Misc` parts, as MIP-0019's publisher. |
| Subscriber | A client that receives every MPE Envelope of a Shard and recognises its own Messages locally. On the ledger lane it joins those parts in ledger order, as MIP-0019's reader. |
| Consumer | A party that acts on a Message: an agent, smart contract, wallet or application. A contract consumes only through a later transaction (DEC-022). |
| Bus Node | A sidecar process, separate from the Midnight node, that joins the GossipSub overlay, validates MPE Envelopes and relays them. |
| Store Node | A Bus Node that also retains MPE Envelopes for back-fill, for 48 hours by default. It stores no chain history. |
| Bus Operator | A party that runs a Bus Node, Store Node or anchorer. An Indexer's operator is an operator in the Foundation's sense, not a Bus Operator. |
| Admission Proof | The proof in the admission slot that authorises one publication within a 60-second admission window and enforces a rate limit through an admission nullifier. |
| Bus Registry | The Compact contract that holds memberships, parameters, the relay list and Anchors. |
| Anchor | A Bus Registry state record holding a root over the identifiers of the MPE Envelopes accepted in one 60-second window, with per-Shard counts. Its copy as a `Misc` event is a notification. |
| Ledger lane | The explicitly selected path that carries an MPE Envelope's sealed body on the ledger as one, four or sixteen `Misc` parts, read through the Indexer. |
| Ledger Adapter | The client-side interface to Midnight state and transaction submission. |
| Indexer | "A service that reads chain data and serves it to wallets and DApps" [@midnight-mps-0028]. Midnight Express uses it as a read path for Bus Registry state and as the carrier of the ledger lane. |

The privacy properties need eight further terms. A *stream* is the audience or sequence of Messages an application cares about ("orders for this market", "messages to this agent"). It is distinct from a Shard, which is only a partition of traffic, and from the Foundation's `@topic` value. At launch both example streams share the single Shard, and a Bus Node sees the Shard's traffic but cannot sort it into the two streams. *Recognition* is the local test a Subscriber runs on each MPE Envelope to decide whether it belongs to one of its streams. Its result is the *recognition outcome*, and the secret that makes the test possible is a *recognition capability*. A *privacy profile* is a named combination of interfaces, client behaviour, assumptions, claims and declared leakage. The *private reception profile* is the one Midnight Express offers by default: the Subscriber receives the complete Shard, recognises locally, and behaves identically on the network whatever the recognition outcome. The *launch profile* is the combination of profiles and features that Midnight Express offers at launch, with the private reception profile as its default. Finally, the *leakage contract* of a profile lists what each observer is permitted to see. It describes protocol interfaces and does not bound what an observer can deduce with outside knowledge.

The general privacy vocabulary follows the terminology of Pfitzmann and Hansen [@2010-pfitzmann-terminology]. Anonymity of a subject means that an attacker cannot sufficiently identify it within a set of subjects, the anonymity set. Unlinkability of two items means that the attacker cannot sufficiently distinguish whether they are related. Undetectability means that the attacker cannot sufficiently tell whether an item exists. Unobservability combines undetectability against uninvolved parties with anonymity against involved ones. Properties about identifying or linking are stated in these terms against a named adversary; content confidentiality and sealed labels concern the sealed bytes themselves.

Where the requirement set makes a property precise, it uses an indistinguishability game in the style of Kuhn and colleagues: the adversary chooses two scenarios that differ only in the protected item, the challenger runs one of them, and the property holds if the adversary cannot tell which ran [@2019-kuhn-privacynotions]. The adversary's *advantage* measures how much better than a coin toss it guesses. At 0 it is right half the time; at 1 it is always right.

The Foundation's only published text on private events is a list of Phase 2 items inside MPS-0005's Phase 1 sections. In the language there is a `@private` field annotation, a `@topic` annotation for filtering, client SDK encryption and decryption, and `@zkp` circuit integration. In the protocol there are "`proven` blocks for commitment binding and `encrypt_for` for encrypted delivery". The Indexer gains a topics column, "wallet-specific decryption with session isolation" and a `RelevantContractEvent` table for per-wallet relevance, and the fee model gains an encryption fee [@midnight-mps-0005]. MPS-0005 says that Phase 2 "is scoped in Part 2 of this document". Part 2 has not been published. The syntax of these constructs is therefore unknown, and so are the recipient key types, the encryption scheme, the topic derivation, the size limit and the fee formula.

The Phase 1 mechanism does fix some things. A private event still shows that a contract emitted something, from which entry point, in which transaction, at what time and with what size. MIP-0019 says so for encrypted `Misc` payloads: "Application encryption may hide payload plaintext but does not hide that metadata or retract disclosed data" [@midnight-mip-0019].

Midnight Express relates to private events in two ways, and it is not an alternative to them: nothing a Publisher's client sends through Midnight Express can stand in for "the contract emitted this field". The first relation is that Midnight Express is a complement. It carries Confidential Messages at rates, sizes, latencies and costs the ledger cannot carry. The ledger's limits are the `bytes_written` budget, which caps the bytes each block writes at 50,000 B, the 256-byte `Misc` payload, about 18 s to finality (about three 6-second blocks) and one proved transaction per emission. Within that byte budget the ledger lane carries about 6.3 class-1 Messages per second for the whole chain. A class-1 sealed body of 1,024 B travels as four `Misc` parts, each a 32-byte name and a 256-byte payload plus MIP-0002's 40-byte overhead, or 328 B; a block's 50,000 B every 6 s is about 8,333 B/s, and 8,333 ÷ (4 × 328) ≈ 6.35. One Shard alone admits 10 MPE Envelopes per second.

The Foundation's documents ask for such a channel. MIP-0011 says that callers "SHOULD deliver" contract-minted coin information "out of band" [@midnight-mip-0011], and MPS-0043 assigns "choosing how the data travels" to a pending problem statement on confidential contract notifications [@midnight-mps-0043]. The custody standard is the counter-case. MIP-0012 requires discovery and recovery "from chain data alone", and Midnight Express is no substitute where that property is required [@midnight-mip-0012]. A wallet restored from a backup must find its funds by reading the chain, and once Store Nodes have dropped a Message after 48 hours, nobody can find it that way.

The second relation is that, under conditions, Midnight Express is a transport for a contract's events, as carried events. It does not replace MPS-0005 Part 2. All the conditions serve one obligation: whatever a Consumer receives through Midnight Express must be checkable, byte for byte, against what the chain records, and it counts for nothing until that check succeeds.

One of the conditions exists because a Midnight transaction can partially succeed. Guaranteed effects apply when it succeeds or partially succeeds, and fallible effects apply only for the segments that succeed. Events follow the outcome of the transcript that produced them [@midnight-mip-0018], so an event from a failed segment describes an action that never took effect. Seven conditions apply:

- The event's bytes, as the ledger will record them, exist off-chain when the transaction is built. compact-runtime captures emitted events during local circuit execution [@midnight-coip-0003]. Whether Phase 2 ciphertext is formed in the client SDK is unknown.
- The chain keeps the binding. Either the ciphertext stays in the transcript, so that Midnight Express only accelerates delivery and hides interest, or the transcript holds a commitment to it. Which of the two Phase 2 permits is unknown.
- The event fits a size class. A single event is at most 1 KiB serialized. With its 32-byte transaction hash and its position it fits the 854-byte payload of class 1 only if the event takes at most 822 B less the bytes of the position; otherwise it needs class 2.
- Recipients can recognise it, today through a stream secret shared by invitation.
- Consumers label it `gossip`, the label for unconfirmed data, until the transaction is finalized and the bytes match authenticated on-chain data, and they count fallible-phase events only if that phase succeeded [@midnight-mip-0018; @midnight-mip-0019].
- The bytes are not re-typed, re-ordered or truncated. The Publisher's signature adds only who forwarded them.
- The event's fee is paid once, on chain, as the gas of its emission and any encryption fee Phase 2 adds. The carrying MPE Envelope still needs an Admission Proof, like any other.

Midnight Express then adds early delivery, and it hides from Bus Nodes and Store Nodes which carried events a Subscriber recognises. Verification through the Indexer still tells the Indexer which contract the Consumer follows (Indexer row of the leakage table). Carriage of a public event hides nothing that the chain does not already show; the four properties then protect only the copy that Midnight Express carries.

Take one public event. The Publisher's client captures the event bytes while building the transaction and publishes them, with the hash and position, as it submits the transaction. The Consumer holds the Message as `gossip` within seconds. About 18 s later it reads the event through the Indexer, compares the bytes, and only then acts.

At the 1 KiB limit, an event and its 32-byte transaction hash already take 1,024 + 32 = 1,056 B, more than the 854-byte payload of class 1, so it travels in class 2, which has 3,926 B of payload.

## The privacy properties

Each property that the requirement set advertises must have an entry in a claim register, recording its game, the adversary classes, the assumptions, the permitted leakage, the supporting evidence and the verification procedure (`MPE-PRV-001`). A property is advertised only when reviewed evidence for the implemented profile exists (`MPE-PRV-030`), and it is withdrawn when verification shows a violation (`MPE-PRV-035`). A property name on its own promises nothing. The properties that follow are those of a Confidential Message and say nothing about a Foundation private event, whose properties MPS-0005 Part 2 will set.

### Content confidentiality

Content confidentiality is defined by the *Content Game*. Two executions differ only in the plaintext of Messages of equal length. Everything the adversary is permitted to observe is identical, and the challenge endpoints and their key establishment are not compromised. An adversary that lacks the decryption secrets must not be able to tell the executions apart with more than negligible advantage (`MPE-PRV-002`). Negligible means that the advantage falls faster than the inverse of any polynomial in the security parameter; at the key lengths in use it is too small to exploit.

In the simplest case one execution carries an order that accepts offer 17 and the other an order that rejects it, both serialized to 200 bytes. In each, a relay sees one 1,544-byte class-1 MPE Envelope and cannot tell the two apart. The property holds against every infrastructure adversary defined below, including the global observer, because cryptography protects content regardless of where the observer stands. For the same reason it does nothing for the observer's view of traffic. The property is close to the first goal of MPS-0043, under which "nobody else, including any node or indexer, can read" disclosed data [@midnight-mps-0043]. It holds only within the cryptographic assumptions and only while no authorised party discloses.

Two narrower properties support it. The first is *sealed labels*: no stream name, recipient address, session identifier or group identifier appears in a cleartext MPE Envelope field (`MPE-FMT-003`). Outside the sealed body, an MPE Envelope carries only its version, size class, Shard, a reserved field, its expiry and the admission slot, so a relay does not see which stream an MPE Envelope belongs to.

The second is *length concealment within a class*. Every MPE Envelope of a size class has the same serialized length, whatever the length of the Message inside (`MPE-FMT-013`), and the client picks the smallest class whose payload capacity holds the Message unless the application names a larger one (`MPE-FMT-014`). A 100-byte Message and an 800-byte Message therefore both travel as 1,544-byte class-1 MPE Envelopes. A 900-byte Message exceeds the 854-byte payload of class 1. It travels as a 4,616-byte class-2 MPE Envelope, and every relay sees that change. Padding hides the payload length within a class, but not the class, the number of MPE Envelopes a Publisher sends, or the variation in GossipSub framing, batching and control traffic that a complete connection transcript shows.

### Conditional interest privacy

The second property concerns Subscribers: infrastructure must not learn which of the MPE Envelopes on a Shard a Subscriber cares about. It is defined by the *Selection Game*. Two executions differ only in the Subscriber's private interests, that is, in its set of recognition keys, within the same set of received Shards. Incoming traffic, the online schedule, transport policy, the application's public actions and external responses are held fixed. The property requires that recognition change nothing the network can see. That covers requests, their sizes, cursors, retries, errors, repair choices (which Shard windows to fetch again from a second source), retention receipts (signed Store Node statements that an MPE Envelope is kept until a deadline) and scheduled transmissions. Under these conditions it holds even against active probes.

It does not hide which Shards the Subscriber receives, nor what the application does afterwards. The property is therefore *conditional*: it holds within a completely received Shard, in the private reception profile (`MPE-PRV-006`). One launch Shard removes any difference between Shards, but participation, connection identity, online schedule and traffic remain visible.

One 60-second window shows what the game asks of a client. At its limit of 10 MPE Envelopes per second, a Shard carries 10 × 60 = 600 MPE Envelopes in the window. Suppose three of them belong to one Subscriber's stream. The Subscriber receives all 600 and tests each one locally; with an illustrative 20 recognition keys, that is 600 × 20 = 12,000 keyed Recognition Tag computations per window, whose cost the proof of concept must measure. The network sees 600 arrivals whether three match or none. The game is lost as soon as the client does something that depends on the three, such as requesting a retention receipt for each of them.

Five client obligations implement the game:

- Recognition, decryption, stream and wallet viewing secrets stay inside the Subscriber's trusted endpoint, the device and process that the Subscriber itself controls (`MPE-PRV-007`).
- No stream identifier, Recognition Tag, recipient, detection key or selector of recognised MPE Envelopes is ever sent to infrastructure (`MPE-PUB-010`).
- Transport behaviour is independent of recognition outcomes (`MPE-PUB-012`).
- Recognition triggers no automatic acknowledgement (`MPE-CON-039`).
- Remotely exported diagnostics carry no interest sets or recognition outcomes (`MPE-PRV-014`).

Two failure rules protect the boundary. If whole-Shard reception exceeds a resource limit, the client reports a failure and does not quietly switch to filtered retrieval. And any alternative path that changes the leakage contract stays inactive until the application explicitly selects the changed profile (`MPE-PRV-013`).

A client that requested a retention receipt or an Anchor inclusion path (the hashes linking an MPE Envelope identifier to an Anchor's root) for each recognised Message would tell the Store Node which Messages matter to it. The specified client avoids this by making such requests per 60-second window. Repair follows the same rule: it retrieves complete differing Shard windows, never only the recognised or missing MPE Envelope identifiers.

GossipSub supplies no proof of the Selection Game. With IHAVE, IWANT and IDONTWANT messages a peer advertises message identifiers it holds, asks for one, or says that it already has one, and these messages, like the subscription traffic, are uniform only as long as the client uses them uniformly. Whether the proof of concept meets the game will be known only once paired transcripts, including repair, retries and errors, are compared under different interest sets. Protocol extensions that let a peer announce which parts of a message it wants, such as GossipSub's partial-message extension, would add interest leakage; they are outside the launch profile unless separately specified and reviewed.

Midnight's own plans take the other side of this trade. MIP-0002 lets a client query standard events by a byte prefix of an indexed field, which "allows the DApp implementer to choose the level of disclosure at the cost of the data returned" [@midnight-mip-0002]. The shorter the prefix, the more the Indexer returns and the less it learns. The topic filtering that MPS-0005 plans for private events reports "99%+ scanning reduction" [@midnight-mps-0005]. Both are selective retrieval, in which a server evaluates something derived from the client's interest and returns less than the whole. Both are therefore reduced-privacy selective profiles, and the requirement set permits such a profile only after the application opts in for each subscription and selects the changed leakage contract (`MPE-PUB-016`, `MPE-PRV-013`).

No Bus Node, Store Node or Bus Registry operation returns who follows a stream (`MPE-PUB-009`), and no key or Recognition Tag filter leaves the client (`MPE-PUB-010`). Together these exclude the per-wallet relevance model by design. MPS-0005 frames the problem that filtering solves: "Fully encrypted events without metadata force wallets to download and trial-decrypt every event on chain." Midnight Express accepts the download and keeps the trial cheap. The Recognition Tag does the work of a topic value and spares the Subscriber a decryption of every MPE Envelope, but it is salted per MPE Envelope and evaluated only by the Subscriber, so no server can group MPE Envelopes by it. A carried event's `@topic` values, once they exist, travel only inside the sealed body.

What MPS-0005's topic filter would reveal is unknown. The published text does not say whether topic values are visible to the Indexer, to chain observers or to neither, nor whether wallet-specific decryption gives the Indexer a decryption capability. Publication of MPS-0005 Part 2, with a statement of what the filtering party learns, would settle both.

### Explicit reactions and disclosure

A Message is useful because someone acts on it, and every action is observable somewhere. A reply, an attachment fetch, a report or a contract transaction can correlate a Consumer with the Message that caused it. If a wallet fetched an attachment the instant it recognised a payment notice, the timing alone would tell the server that a Message for that wallet had just arrived. Midnight Express therefore requires that recognition alone start no external action. The application must explicitly authorise each Message-dependent action (`MPE-PUB-047`) and each disclosure of plaintext outside the authorised audience (`MPE-PRV-015`). The resulting activity is declared leakage; it lies outside the Selection Game, and Midnight Express makes no claim about it.

### Claims held back

*Recipient-key hiding* is named but deliberately withheld. It would mean that an adversary who knows the candidate recipients' public keys cannot tell which of them an MPE Envelope is for, which is key privacy in the sense of Bellare and colleagues [@2001-bellare-keyprivacy]. Encrypting the content and omitting the address do not establish it. A ciphertext can carry structure that depends on the recipient's key, and an adversary holding the candidate keys can test for it. The claim stays unadvertised until a review of the exact wrapper and the Recognition Tag lifecycle establishes it (`MPE-PRV-004`).

At launch, Midnight Express uses symmetric stream secrets distributed by invitation, so the question first arises with a public-key profile for first contact (a Publisher's first Message to a recipient with whom it shares no stream secret). No such profile is part of the launch design. It would encrypt to a recipient's Midnight shielded encryption key and would follow `encrypt_for` once MPS-0005 Part 2 specifies its key type.

## Adversary classes

The requirement set defines five adversary classes by capability, not by identity, and one Bus Operator can fall into several.

**R, local infrastructure observer.** A single Bus Node, Store Node, admission issuer, Indexer or wallet service provider. It sees its own connections, requests, MPE Envelopes, operational state and service records.

**C, colluding infrastructure.** Any set of infrastructure parties that pool their observations, including enrolment, issuance and ingress records. The common phrase "colluding minority" suggests a threshold below which privacy holds, and the requirement set rejects that reading. A numerical minority of relays supplies no independent privacy guarantee, because nothing in a gossip overlay requires an honest majority for confidentiality, and the joins a coalition can make depend on where its members sit, not on how many there are. Take an admission issuer that knows which customer bought an allowance and an ingress Bus Node that sees which connection spent it. Neither alone can name the Publisher of an MPE Envelope. Together they can, although they are only two parties in the whole overlay.

**G, global passive observer.** It sees the timing, sizes and counts of traffic at endpoints and between infrastructure nodes, online intervals and all public ledger activity, and it may obtain infrastructure records. A national network operator, or a coalition of transit providers, approximates it.

**A, active network adversary.** It injects valid or malformed traffic, replays, delays, drops, probes, partitions, eclipses and creates identities, and it may also hold C's observations.

**I, insider or compromised endpoint.** It holds an authorised recognition capability, receives plaintext, controls application behaviour, or has obtained an endpoint's secrets. An auditor given a stream secret belongs here.

Two parties are not overlay infrastructure. The *Indexer* is an R-class observer with a particular view: it sees the queries of the wallets and DApps it serves, and it serves public chain data. A *chain observer* is anyone who reads the public ledger. Reading needs no privileged position, so in practice every party in every class is also a chain observer.

## What each adversary learns

Each observer has a different permitted view under the leakage contract, and a different part of each Message stays protected from it. The rows are inferences from the specified fields and messages, confirmed against code where a field exists in code, and they do not bound deductions that use outside knowledge. Every applicable privacy profile must carry these rows in its leakage declaration (`MPE-PRV-016` to `MPE-PRV-023`). The last row is a special case of the insider row, which every leakage declaration must state separately. The first four relay entries identify participants and support estimates of origin.

| Observer | Learns | Does not learn (under the stated assumptions) |
|---|---|---|
| Single relay (Bus Node) | If it is the ingress node, the submitting connection. Identities and IP addresses of its connected peers, authenticated by the transport; which Shards each peer subscribes to, with the network prefix and protocol major version carried in each GossipSub topic name; arrival times, forwarding decisions and traffic counts; every MPE Envelope's bytes, identifier, size class and expiry; equality of Recognition Tags if a Recognition Tag repeats; Admission Proof metadata; which message identifiers each peer advertises, requests or declines (IHAVE, IWANT, IDONTWANT), and when; its own per-peer score state; the public key, addresses, supported protocols and implementation metadata of each peer if libp2p's Identify protocol is enabled. | Sealed plaintext and sealed labels; Subscribers' recognition outcomes. |
| Store Node | Everything a relay learns, plus the client's connection and service account if any, online intervals, Shards received, windows requested, cursors, catch-up volume and repair requests. A selective profile also exposes its selectors. | Plaintext without secrets; selection within a Shard, in the private reception profile only. |
| Admission issuer (where one exists) | The customer or funding relationship, the allowance, issuance time, credential identifiers, and MPE Envelope identifiers if issuance binds to them. Joined with publication records, this can identify a Publisher. | Plaintext, and Subscriber secrets it was never given. |
| Indexer | Public chain data; which contract addresses and filters a client queries; the client's connection, cursors and request schedule. A client that reads the ledger lane filters by the lane contract address and the `Misc` type only (`MPE-CON-053`), so the Indexer learns that it follows the lane. Assisted wallet scanning also exposes the viewing key the wallet hands over. | Off-chain plaintext without keys; which sealed stream a client selects among the ledger-lane MPE Envelopes, all of which a lane client downloads. |
| Chain observer | Bus Registry changes, registrations and revocations; transaction times and hashes; contract addresses, entry points, public transcripts and declared effects; public fees, DUST nullifiers and commitments; Anchors, their 60-second cadence, the anchorer that posted each and their per-Shard MPE Envelope counts; the sealed body, size class and submitting transaction of every ledger-lane MPE Envelope, kept on chain beyond its expiry; public funding and contract reactions, with the consumption nullifier and a coarse expiry bound of each reaction. | Sealed off-chain plaintext and undisclosed witnesses, including which anchored MPE Envelope a contract reaction concerns. |
| Colluding infrastructure | The union of its members' views, and the joins between them: enrolment to issuance to ingress, grouping by repeated Recognition Tags, first-seen estimates of origin, intersections across time, and chances to probe or omit selectively. | Content, while keys are uncompromised; selection, only under the complete Selection Game. No bound depends on the coalition being a minority. |
| Global passive observer | Who participates; uploads, downloads, online intervals, timing, sizes, counts, catch-up patterns, public ledger activity and application reactions; the source and relationship correlations that follow from these. | Content, while keys are uncompromised. Conditional interest privacy gives it no general anonymity. |
| Insider or compromised endpoint | Authorised plaintext, recognition capabilities, the traffic it can recognise, group information the application supplies, and the compromised state. All of it can be copied or disclosed. | Nothing already authorised or compromised; unrelated secrets keep their own boundaries. |
| Auditor or other delegate given a stream secret | Every Message of that stream it can obtain: all later Messages until the stream moves to a new secret, and every earlier MPE Envelope of the stream that it, or anyone it can ask, archived. It can recognise the stream's traffic on the Shard, and so learns the stream's volume and timing. The capability is coarse and cannot be revoked: ending it requires a new stream secret distributed to every other member, and what the delegate has read stays read. | Other streams, whose secrets it was not given; any narrower grant, such as one Message or one period, which this instrument cannot express. |

GossipSub sends a SUBSCRIBE message for every joined topic to every connected peer, so Shard membership is visible to neighbours by construction.^[libp2p/rust-libp2p, protocols/gossipsub/src/behaviour.rs, function `subscribe`, loop sending `RpcOut::Subscribe` to all connected peers; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] Shard topic names have the form `/mpe/<g>/1/shard/<i>`, where `g` is the first eight bytes of the genesis hash, so they also disclose the network prefix and the protocol major version. Publishing under GossipSub's `StrictNoSign` policy builds messages without the `from`, `seqno`, `signature` and `key` fields.^[libp2p/specs, pubsub/README.md, section on signature policy, option `StrictNoSign`; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] That removes an explicit origin stamp. It leaves the ingress observation, the authenticated connection identity and the timing of forwarding, because a libp2p identity authenticates the connected endpoint, not the sealed Publisher, and later relays see their forwarding peers.

The rust-libp2p gossipsub release 0.50 does not reject an incoming message that carries only the `key` field; the check was added in 0.51.^[libp2p/rust-libp2p, protocols/gossipsub/CHANGELOG.md, entry 0.51.0, "Reject messages carrying a `key` field in `ValidationMode::Anonymous`"; master branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] A Bus Node on 0.50 is therefore not fully `StrictNoSign`, so the proof of concept must use 0.51 or later or add the check itself. Separate sidecar keys avoid reusing the Midnight node's identity, but a shared host, address and traffic can still link the two processes.

The Indexer's contract event filter requires a contract address, so a client reading Bus Registry activity or the ledger lane through the Indexer tells it which contract it follows.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-indexer, indexer-api/graphql/schema-v4.graphql, input `ContractEventFilter`, field `contractAddress: HexEncoded!`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] The Indexer's wallet component trial-decrypts each new transaction against the viewing key of every active wallet.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-indexer, docs/architecture.md, description of the `wallet-indexer` component; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] A viewing key is the secret that lets its holder recognise and read a wallet's shielded transactions without being able to spend from it. A wallet that uses the component has handed its viewing capability to the Indexer's operator, and MPS-0043 notes the same of the shielded wallet's single viewing key [@midnight-mps-0043].

The chain-observer row includes the per-Shard counts that every Anchor carries (`MPE-PRV-038`). The payload always carries eight count fields, zero for unused Shards. They reveal per-Shard volume per minute to anyone, and they matter more once there is more than one Shard: if one minute's Anchor records 600 MPE Envelopes on a Shard and the next records 3, every reader of the chain learns that activity on that Shard fell, without seeing any content.

Three contracts of Midnight Express exist as exploratory Compact code for the ledger interface, compiled with toolchain 0.35.0 (language 0.27.0, a ledger-9 release candidate): the Bus Registry, a ledger-lane contract, and a consumer contract, which shows how a contract acts on a Message through a later transaction (a *consumer reaction*). Their circuit sizes are read from the compiled output and their fees derived from the ledger cost model. Nothing was run on a Midnight network, so the disclosures that follow come from the compiled public transcripts, and the design does not depend on these contracts being final. A circuit of size k fits in 2^k rows, so k = 18 allows 2^18 = 262,144 rows. Fees are paid in DUST, Midnight's non-transferable fee resource, which held NIGHT generates over about a week up to a cap of 5 DUST per NIGHT.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-ledger, ledger/src/dust.rs, constant `INITIAL_DUST_PARAMETERS`; main branch, accessed 1 October 2026.] Genesis prices are ledger 9's starting fee prices, and the wallet's margin is a factor of about 1.25 against later price movement.

A registration discloses the membership period and the registrant's identity commitment. It is a public transaction of about 8.7 KB, costing about 0.246 DUST at genesis prices, or 0.308 with the wallet's margin. A revocation discloses the period, the root its evidence proves against, the leaf index, the identity commitment and a reason code. Each change to the relay list, a parameter, the pause state or the steward (the party that governs the Bus Registry) emits a governance `Misc` event with the action, a reason and its subject.

An Anchor post discloses the anchorer's relay key (a hash of its libp2p peer identifier), the window, the window root and the eight counts. Each post costs about 0.195 DUST at genesis prices and needs a proof of a k = 18 circuit, so one Anchor per minute costs about 1,440 × 0.195 ≈ 280 DUST per day. At 5 ÷ 7 ≈ 0.71 DUST per NIGHT per day, 280 DUST per day is the generation of about 280 ÷ 0.71 ≈ 390 NIGHT (derived).

A ledger-lane MPE Envelope is written as one, four or sixteen public `Misc` parts, so its part count reveals its size class. The counts follow from the 256-byte `Misc` payload: sealed bodies of 256, 1,024 and 4,096 B fill 1, 4 and 16 payloads. A class-3 body is refused on the ledger lane before any transaction is built (`MPE-FMT-044`).

A consumer reaction discloses an Anchor-tree root, a coarse expiry bound chosen by the prover, the consumption nullifier and the action, and keeps the MPE Envelope identifier, window, Shard, inclusion paths and Message secret private. The bound exists because the reaction's circuit cannot read the block time, only compare a value with it, and the comparison puts that value in the public transcript. An expiry is set at creation, at most 48 hours ahead (`MPE-FMT-028`). Comparing the sealed expiry with block time would therefore publish it, and at the full lifetime the expiry less 48 hours is the creation second. The compiled circuit instead compares block time with a bound the prover chooses, and checks privately that the MPE Envelope's anchored window plus 48 hours reaches it. The creation minute then lies between the bound less 48 hours and the newest window under the disclosed Anchor-tree root: almost 48 hours wide if the bound is set just past the current block time. A reaction also carries two contract calls, the consumer's and a read of the Bus Registry, so a chain observer sees that it relies on an Anchor.

Midnight transactions carry no Substrate signer. The node validates them as unsigned extrinsics, which in Substrate means submitted calls that carry no account signature.^[midnightntwrk/midnight-node, pallets/midnight/src/lib.rs, function `validate_unsigned` for `Call::send_mn_transaction`; main branch, ledger 9 pinned, accessed 1 October 2026.] For the same reason the compiled Bus Registry authorises its steward and anchorers by a hash preimage proved in circuit. The missing signer is not evidence of anonymity, because public transcripts, fee and funding information, ingress observations and sparse activity can still link a transaction to its submitter, and the requirement set forbids presenting it as publisher or payer unlinkability (`MPE-PRV-025`).

The fields listed come from the ledger-9 code that the node pins. Contract events, and with them Anchor notifications and the ledger lane, need ledger 9 and a Compact toolchain of 0.33 or later. Mainnet ran ledger 8.0 as of 4 August 2026, and MIP-0002 is live on Stagenet; the current mainnet status is unknown until the deployed ledger version is checked. The published documentation describes the earlier ledger generation, in which contract events are not available.

The proof of concept will use a stand-in admission mechanism whose proof reveals the member's secrets to every Bus Node. In it, every relay can link every MPE Envelope to the membership that published it, so no result from the proof of concept can support a claim about admission privacy.

The intended mechanism is a rate-limiting-nullifier proof against a membership root on the Bus Registry, which would hide which member published an MPE Envelope. In such a proof the member shows that it holds the secret behind some leaf of the membership tree without saying which leaf. It also reveals an admission nullifier derived from that secret, the admission window, the size class and a credit index (`MPE-ECO-017`). In such a scheme each publication also carries a Shamir share of the admission secret. One share alone does not reveal the secret, but two shares from different messages under the same admission nullifier reconstruct it, so a member that publishes beyond its limit gives that secret away [@2022-taheri-spam-protected-gossip]. The compiled Bus Registry already checks the same relation in its revocation circuit, using the native field and `transientHash`. The production proof system, its size against the 512-byte admission slot and its proving cost are unknown. Even with such a proof, the ingress observation remains, and so does the issuer row wherever an issuer exists. A remote prover would add a row of its own, since a client that delegates proof generation hands the prover its private witness [@midnight-mps-0004]. The requirement set therefore keeps the admission secret inside the client's trust boundary unless the prover is attested.

Handing a stream secret to an auditor is a coarse, irrevocable viewing capability, equivalent to the custody contract's viewing capability in MIP-0012. There, a single encryption secret "reveals the contract's entire shielded past and future until `enc_key` is rotated", and the delegate "retains whatever it has already read" [@midnight-mip-0012]. An auditor given a market's order-stream secret in March can read every January MPE Envelope of that stream that any Store Node or archive kept, and every later one until the members move to a new secret. MPS-0043 asks for the opposite: a grant that "covers only what it names: one item, one direction, one counterparty, or one period", with a stated lifetime and without "handing over a long-term key" [@midnight-mps-0043]. Midnight Express at launch provides no scoped grant. A per-Message or per-period disclosure object would combine the Message secret, the signed statement, an Anchor inclusion path and a discloser signature, so that the reader can check the Message against the chain and know who disclosed it. That object remains a requirement still to be written, with an explicit non-claim for every MPS-0043 goal it does not meet.

## What Midnight Express does not claim

The launch profile labels the following properties as unclaimed (`MPE-PRV-024` and `MPE-PRV-026` to `MPE-PRV-029`).

- Publisher anonymity and publisher-message unlinkability. The ingress Bus Node sees the submitting connection, and a coalition or global observer can estimate origin from first sightings. No GossipSub configuration removes these observations.
- General recipient-message unlinkability. Interest privacy holds within a complete Shard. It does not hide Shard membership, and it does not hide reactions.
- Relationship privacy. Nothing hides that a Publisher and a Consumer interact when their activity can be correlated in time or through the ledger.
- Timing and volume privacy. There is no cover traffic and no batching at launch, and per-Shard counts are on the chain.
- Hidden participation. Running the client, joining a Shard and registering a membership are all observable.
- Forward secrecy, metadata forward secrecy and post-compromise security. Launch stream secrets are static.
- Post-quantum confidentiality. Even a future hybrid payload mechanism would not make the whole system post-quantum, because its signatures, Admission Proofs and ledger cryptography would remain classical.
- Erasure from adversarial archives. The 48-hour expiry is a retention rule for honest services. Anyone can keep a captured MPE Envelope, with its metadata, indefinitely, and ledger-lane bodies stay on chain.
- Anonymity from a stem, a first hop that forwards an MPE Envelope before it spreads. The specified client submits each MPE Envelope to one of two dialled Bus Nodes chosen at random. Any source-attribution benefit of this is an unproven heuristic until an analysis covers the actual construction and adversary.
- Deniability. Each Message carries a publisher signature inside the seal, discussed below.
- Scoped disclosure. The only disclosure instruments at launch are plaintext handed over by an authorised party and a whole stream secret.

Two broader statements hold as well. "Bus Operators cannot read the content" is true within the cryptographic assumptions. "Bus Operators cannot classify anything" and "Bus Operators cannot be compelled" are not, because metadata, insiders, endpoint compromise and legal process lie outside the cryptographic claim. And no design can make recipients the only parties able to reveal content: the Publisher already knows the Message, and an authorised recipient can copy it.

## Limits from the literature

Each non-claim corresponds to a result in the literature that would make a stronger claim either false or expensive at the operating point of Midnight Express. A quantitative bound from a paper becomes a guarantee only after its assumptions and variables have been mapped to the implemented profile (`MPE-PRV-034`).

### The anonymity trilemma

Das, Meiser, Mohammadi and Kate prove that, against a global passive adversary, an anonymous communication protocol cannot have strong anonymity, low bandwidth overhead and low latency overhead at once [@2017-das-trilemma]. In their protocol-friendly model, N users are globally synchronised and each sends exactly one real message over N rounds, one user per round. The protocol may delay a message by up to ℓ rounds and may have each user send up to β noise messages per round. Theorem 1 bounds the adversary's advantage from below: δ ≥ 1 − f(ℓ), where f(ℓ) = min(1, (ℓ + βNℓ)/(N − 1)). Theorem 2 draws the consequence. With ℓ < N and βN ≥ 1, no protocol achieves strong anonymity (negligible advantage) if 2ℓβ < 1 − ε(η), where ε(η) = 1/η^d for a positive constant d and security parameter η [@2017-das-trilemma, sec. V].

At launch Midnight Express sends no cover traffic, so β = 0. Theorem 2 then does not apply, because it assumes βN ≥ 1, and Theorem 1 gives the bound directly: δ ≥ 1 − ℓ/(N − 1). Take an illustrative 1,000 users and a tolerated delay of 10 rounds. The adversary's advantage is then at least 1 − 10/999 ≈ 0.99. Reaching the boundary of Theorem 2 at that latency needs 2 × 10 × β ≥ 1, so β ≥ 0.05: that is 0.05 × 1,000 = 50 noise messages per round against one real message, a fifty-fold bandwidth overhead. The theorem states a necessary condition, not a construction, and meeting it does not make a protocol anonymous.

Two cautions govern the transfer to Midnight Express. Model rounds are not seconds; a round is the time in which one user's message is due, so mapping it to wall time needs the real arrival rate. And GossipSub's duplicate forwarding is not noise in the theorem's sense, because every copy carries the same real message along predictable mesh edges. At 10 MPE Envelopes per second per Shard, with a delivery target of seconds and no cover traffic, Midnight Express sits at the corner of the trilemma that gives up anonymity against a global observer. It therefore claims none.

### The anonymity of gossip

Guerraoui, Kermarrec, Kucherenko, Pinot and Voitovych use differential privacy to measure how well gossip hides its source [@2023-guerraoui-inherent-anonymity-gossiping]. In their model, f curious nodes run the protocol honestly and report everything they see; messages are neither signed nor encrypted in a way that reveals the source, and the adversary does not know when dissemination started. A gossip protocol is ε-differentially private if, for any two possible sources, the distributions of the adversary's observations differ by at most a factor e^ε. Theorem 5 states that on any undirected connected graph with f > 1 curious nodes, every gossip protocol has ε ≥ ln(f − 1). Moreover, if the graph's vertex connectivity κ(G) is at most f, no finite ε is possible against an adversary that chooses where its nodes sit [@2023-guerraoui-inherent-anonymity-gossiping, sec. 4.2]. The proof uses the simplest estimator: guess that the first honest node to contact the curious set is the source. Applied to one MPE Envelope, the coalition notes which honest Bus Node first delivered it to any member and names that Bus Node as the origin.

With three curious nodes, ε ≥ ln 2 ≈ 0.69, so one candidate source can be twice as likely as another. With ten, ε ≥ ln 9 ≈ 2.2, a factor of nine. Here f counts nodes, not a fraction, so the bound does not weaken as the network grows.

The connectivity condition is sharper still. Vertex connectivity is the smallest number of nodes whose removal splits the graph. GossipSub maintains each node's mesh for a GossipSub topic between D_low = 6 and D_high = 12 peers, with target D = 8 [@2020-gossipsub-v11-spec]. These are maintenance bounds, not a hard limit on neighbours: the degree can fall below D_low while peers are scarce, and gossip to non-mesh peers, publication to fanout peers, explicit peers and opportunistic grafting widen the set of peers that see a node's traffic. The vertex connectivity of a graph is never larger than the smallest number of connections any node holds, and in this profile that number is small. An adversary that holds all the overlay neighbours of a Publisher's ingress Bus Node separates it from the rest of the graph, and the theorem then allows no finite guarantee. If those neighbours were only the eight mesh peers at the target degree, eight Bus Nodes would suffice, whatever the size of the overlay. In the specified system the point is moot for the ingress node itself, which sees the submitting connection directly (relay row of the table).

Dandelion++ shows what a dedicated design achieves under favourable assumptions [@2018-fanti-dandelionpp]. It first passes each message along a short stem of single forwards and only then lets it spread, so that the point where spreading begins is not the source. Its analysis scores spies holding a fraction p of nodes by recall, the share of all sources they name correctly, and precision, the share of their guesses that are correct. The paper cites a lower bound for honest-but-curious spies: no spreading mechanism holds their expected recall below p or their expected precision below p² [@2018-fanti-dandelionpp, sec. 3.2]. Its *first-spy estimator* is the estimator of Guerraoui and colleagues above.

Theorem 1 of Dandelion++ bounds the adversary, not the protocol. On a random 4-regular anonymity graph that the adversary does not know, no estimator's expected precision exceeds eight times the first-spy estimator's plus 6p² and terms of order p³, so a first-spy measurement understates the strongest adversary by at most that margin [@2018-fanti-dandelionpp, sec. 4.1]. The model targets mass deanonymisation, not targeted attacks, and it excludes ISP-level adversaries, which can eclipse a node. The requirement set's first-spy measurement covers coalitions of 1, 3, 5, 10 and 20 percent (`MPE-VER-027`). By the recall bound, at p = 0.05 even an optimal mechanism lets the coalition attribute at least 5 percent of publications in expectation (derived). A one-hop stem on a GossipSub mesh does not inherit Dandelion++'s results. The specified one-hop stem is therefore left to the proof of concept, which will measure first-spy precision and recall at those coalition sizes, and it is not claimed.

### The cost of receiver privacy

Midnight Express hides a Subscriber's interests by making it download the whole Shard. The alternatives move the work elsewhere; none removes it.

Private information retrieval shows why. A single server answering a PIR query must touch every bit of the database, because otherwise it learns which records the client does not want [@2022-henzinger-simplepir]. SimplePIR, the fastest single-server scheme its authors report, reaches 10 GB/s per core, close to memory bandwidth, but its client must first download a 121 MB hint to query a 1 GB database [@2022-henzinger-simplepir]. Each Shard's byte cap is 64 KiB, or 65,536 B, per second. A Shard retained for 48 hours at that cap holds 65,536 B/s × 172,800 s ≈ 11.3 GB, so at 10 GB/s each private query costs a Store Node about 1.1 core-seconds, for every query from every client.

Oblivious message retrieval delegates detection to an untrusted server, which learns nothing about which messages are addressed to whom, even if everyone except the recipient colludes [@2021-liu-omr]. The cost moves to the server: about 0.065 s per message on a four-vCPU cloud machine, about USD 1.02 per million messages scanned per recipient, and a one-time detection key of about 129 MB [@2021-liu-omr]. At one Shard's 10 MPE Envelopes per second, one recipient's detection occupies 10 × 0.065 = 0.65 of such a machine continuously. A day's 10 × 86,400 = 864,000 MPE Envelopes cost about 0.864 × USD 1.02 ≈ USD 0.88 per recipient.

Fuzzy message detection trades privacy for cost explicitly. A detector holding a detection key with false-positive rate p returns every true match plus about a fraction p of other messages. The recipient's anonymity set is the false positives, so a smaller p means a smaller download and a smaller set. At p = 1/100 over one day of one Shard, a recipient would download its own Messages plus about 864,000 / 100 = 8,640 of the others, and the detector would know that the recipient's Messages are among the set it returned, about 8,640 plus the recipient's own. Penumbra's variant lets senders choose the rate, so that the filtered stream stays near a target size; as its specification notes, senders, not the recipient, then decide the precision, and nothing enforces a floor [@2022-penumbra-fmd]. For this reason Midnight Express keeps fuzzy detection disabled (DEC-020, `MPE-CRY-037`) and uses authenticated invitations for first contact. Selective reception, including any filtering of the MIP-0002 or MPS-0005 kind, is admitted only as a separately labelled profile that the application chooses (`MPE-PUB-016`, `MPE-PRV-013`).

Against these figures, whole-Shard download is the choice that keeps the Selection Game intact with no per-query or per-recipient work on any server. The client pays in bandwidth. At the Shard's byte cap the download is 65,536 B/s × 86,400 s ≈ 5.7 GB per day, affordable for a server, an agent or a desktop wallet and far beyond a mobile data budget. The proof of concept will measure the download at its baseline traffic mix (Chapter 9). Interest privacy for phones is not claimed at launch.

### Forward secrecy, post-compromise security and deniability

Forward secrecy would mean that a key stolen today does not open traffic sent before the theft. Midnight Express seals Messages under static stream secrets at launch, and static-key encryption has no forward secrecy against recipient compromise. The HPKE standard states that compromise of a recipient's long-term secret lets an attacker decrypt past ciphertexts, because only long-term secrets are used on the recipient's side [@2022-barnes-rfc9180, sec. 9.7.4]. The same holds for a shared symmetric stream secret. Relays, coalitions and global observers can all archive MPE Envelopes, so the exposure is not limited to the 48-hour retention window: a stream secret stolen next year opens every MPE Envelope of that stream that anyone kept.

Ratcheting protocols reduce this exposure only if keys are erased. The Double Ratchet derives a fresh key per message and requires deletion of used keys and, after a bounded time, of keys stored for messages that have not yet arrived [@2016-signal-double-ratchet-spec, sec. 8.4]. A broadcast stream with offline Subscribers and a 48-hour back-fill window must keep keys at least that long.

Post-compromise security, recovery after a full state compromise, has a further limit. Cremers, Medinger and Naska prove that communication systems that must tolerate certain kinds of state loss cannot give end users full post-compromise security, whatever session-management layer they use, and such state loss happens in practice [@2024-cremers-pcs-impossibility]. Midnight Express is such a system: clients crash, restore from backups and join from several devices. Session profiles with narrower, reviewed guarantees can be added later (DEC-002). The launch profile claims none of these properties.

Deniability fails for a structural reason. Each Message carries, inside the seal, an Ed25519 signature by an authorised publisher key, and the signed statement includes the stream, sequence number, creation time and a hash of the payload. The signature gives Consumers and contracts the authentication they need: a contract can check in circuit that an authorised Publisher produced the Message, and without that check, consumption by a contract has no authority.

The exploratory consumer contract shows what is missing without it. It proves that some anchored MPE Envelope exists, in a circuit of 77,905 rows (k = 17, read from the compiled circuit; never run), and derives a consumption nullifier from a private Message secret. Nothing in the circuit binds that secret to that MPE Envelope, so a holder of the inclusion proof could react several times with different secrets. The signature circuit that would bind them was not written or compiled. Compact's `ed25519Verify` requires an experimental proof-system feature, and MIP-0013 rejects Ed25519 in circuits because emulated field arithmetic is costly [@midnight-mip-0013]. The requirement set therefore weighs an optional JubJub Schnorr publisher profile, and the proof of concept will compile and cost both circuits before one is chosen.

Either way, a digital signature is non-repudiable. Borisov, Goldberg and Brewer built off-the-record messaging around exactly this distinction: a signature lets anyone convince a third party that the signer sent the message, while a MAC authenticates only to its holder [@2004-borisov-otr, sec. 3.2–3.3]. Any recipient can therefore hand an opened Message and its signature to a third party who knows the publisher's key. If the members of a stream shared a MAC key instead, any member could have computed the tag, and a tag shown to an outsider would prove only that some member wrote the Message. Collins, Colombo and Huguenin-Dumittan add that, even where cryptographic deniability exists, it can be empty in practice once a judge sees device state and server records [@2023-collins-real-deniability]. Midnight Express chose publisher authentication over deniability. A deniable profile would need MAC-based authentication among stream members and would lose verification by contracts.

## The definition

**Private event.** A contract event, emitted by a Compact circuit during transaction execution, some of whose fields are encrypted for chosen recipients and bound to the transaction by a commitment (a short value that binds the ciphertext without revealing it) that the proof checks, as planned for Phase 2 of MPS-0005. A private event is defined by Midnight, not by this design.

**Confidential Message.** A Message that a Publisher seals into a fixed-size MPE Envelope and delivers through Midnight Express, such that:

1. Content confidentiality. While the challenge endpoints and their key establishment are not compromised, no party without the stream's secrets can distinguish two Messages of equal length with more than negligible advantage (the Content Game). That covers a relay, a Store Node, the Indexer, a chain observer, a colluding set of Bus Operators, an active network adversary and a global passive observer.
2. Sealed labels. No stream name, recipient address, session identifier or group identifier appears in a cleartext field. In clear, an MPE Envelope shows only its version, size class, Shard, a reserved field, its expiry and the admission slot.
3. Conditional interest privacy. In the private reception profile, a Subscriber that receives its complete Shards and recognises locally behaves identically on the network whatever its interests (the Selection Game), so no infrastructure party learns from its traffic which MPE Envelopes it wanted.
4. No silent change. No alternative retrieval path, overload path, ledger lane or application reaction changes this leakage without an explicit choice by the application.

A Confidential Message is not a contract event. It is authenticated by its Publisher's signature and committed by an Anchor, not by contract execution.

**Carried event.** A Confidential Message whose payload is the unchanged bytes of a contract event, public or private, together with the transaction hash and position that let a Consumer verify it against the chain. The event keeps its own transcript binding and its authority as the contract author's claim. A Consumer labels it `gossip`, that is, unconfirmed, until the transaction is finalized and the bytes match authenticated on-chain data, and counts a fallible-phase event only if that phase succeeded.

A Confidential Message does not hide who participates, which Shards a client uses, who submitted an MPE Envelope to its first relay, when and how much anyone publishes, which parties interact, or what an application does on the ledger in response. Nor does it have any of the properties that the launch profile labels unclaimed. These limits hold until a separately reviewed profile, with its own claim register and evidence (`MPE-PRV-037`), says otherwise.

The chapter closes with the formal Midnight Problem Statement MPS-xxxx, "Confidential Message and Private Event Delivery for Contracts, Agents and Wallets", which follows this definition and states the same problem in the Foundation's format.
